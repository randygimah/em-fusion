EM Fusion in plain English

What the system is for, what has actually been built so far, and a worked example that follows one real aircraft report all the way through the machinery that exists today.

The problem

The electromagnetic spectrum is crowded. Aircraft broadcast their position, radios talk, radars sweep, satellites send navigation signals down. Somebody watching all of that at once has to answer a question that sounds simple and is not: is something wrong here, and if so, what?

The difficulty is that the interesting cases all look alike from a distance. An aircraft's reported position going stale might mean its navigation is being jammed — or it might mean the receiving station missed a few messages. A radio sensor screaming noise might mean genuine interference — or a faulty sensor. Deciding between those is judgement, and it is judgement made under time pressure with incomplete evidence.

EM Fusion is a prototype that helps with that judgement. It pulls several kinds of observation into one shape, looks for deviations from normal using rules that are written down rather than learned, packages the evidence, and asks an AI model to propose an explanation — one that is checked, line by line, against the evidence it was given.

It never decides. Every hypothesis it produces goes to a person, who confirms it, rejects it, or asks for more. That is the whole design stance: decision support, not autonomous decision-making.

The pipeline, and how far along it we are

Data moves through nine stages. The first four carry real data and the last one is connected, and for most of the project's life everything between them was empty — the two ends meeting around a missing middle. The middle then filled in: the system measures what normal looks like, has a rule for noticing when something is not, keeps what it finds, can ask a map where those findings happened and which of them belong together, gathers them into packages, checks each package's evidence against the store rather than taking it on trust, and turns a package into a written explanation — the first thing that produces one having no AI in it at all, for reasons that turn out to be the whole point. This week the chain was joined up end to end and the screen was connected to it. Until now every one of those stages had been exercised only by measurement scripts: the rules ran, the figures were real, and nothing in the running system called them. An hour's detection now happens as one pass inside the application, what it concludes is written down, and the screen shows it. The gap that closes is smaller than it sounds and worse than it sounds — for seven weeks this system could compute all of the above and a person looking at it could see none of it.

Seventy-two of them are finished. Several were added along the way because they had never been written down at all — one a rule the detection requirements assumed existed, another a question two capabilities both referred to as though somebody else was answering it.

What has actually been built

Five things. The third is the point of the project; the other four exist to feed it or to keep it honest.

An hour of real air traffic, reproducible from a recipe
A single hour of aircraft position broadcasts over the Low Countries and western Germany, filtered to a fixed box and archived locally. The data itself is not in the repository — the licence forbids passing it on — so what is committed is the recipe: where to get it, the checksum it must match, and the tooling to rebuild it in one command.
A translator for that source
It reads a slice of the archive and hands out records in a neutral shape. It does not decide whether a record is any good, does not throw anything away, and does not know a database exists. Every one of those is somebody else's job on purpose, so the second and third sources inherit the behaviour rather than reimplementing it.
The Data Abstraction Layer
One shape that every source becomes, whatever it started as. It checks each record has what it needs, sets aside the ones that do not along with the reason, stamps every survivor with where it came from and under which run, and replaces real aircraft identifiers with stable fakes. This is the core of the project's first research question.
A place to put it all, and the thing that puts it there
A geographic database with seven tables covering the whole pipeline, created by versioned migration scripts so any empty database can be brought to the current shape with one command, twice in a row, without complaint. And the piece that writes into it, which had been designed a long time before the database existed — see below for whether that gamble paid off.
A command that runs the whole thing
One instruction replays the archived hour from disk, through the front door, into the database. Before it existed the only way to move data end to end was to run the test suite — which meant the project's headline timing figure had been taken with a script that no longer existed, and could not be checked.
One command surface over the whole thing
The system runs in containers, and every operation — start, migrate, test, lint, smoke-test — is one make target. The tests run inside the container against a real database, so a green result on a laptop means something on any other machine.

Where the data comes from

Aircraft continuously broadcast their identity, position, altitude and speed. A volunteer network of ground receivers collects those broadcasts worldwide and publishes an archive. One hour of it — midday on 27 June 2022 — is this project's replayed real data.

A box was drawn around a region and everything outside it discarded. That choice matters more than it sounds, because the same box constrains three separate things: which aircraft reports enter the system, which map tiles have to be prepared, and the geographic space in which every simulated incident will later be placed.

The box is roughly square on the ground — about 555 km across and 556 km tall — which is why it is drawn square here. It holds 133,134 positioned aircraft reports across the hour — one every ten seconds per aircraft, from 948 distinct airframes.

The criterion we expected to use did not work

The plan was to choose the box on coverage quality: pick a region where the receiver network is dense enough that a real gap in reporting is rare, so that later, when we deliberately inject a fake gap, we can tell the two apart. Eight candidate regions across Europe and the north-eastern United States were measured. Every single one came back essentially perfect — over 99.97% of consecutive reports arriving exactly on the ten-second beat. The measure could not choose between them because there was nothing to choose.

Getting to that conclusion needed the measurement corrected first, and the correction is the interesting part. The naive version reported gaps of up to 3,140 seconds in a file only 3,600 seconds long, which reads as catastrophe. It was not. Those were aircraft flying out of the box and back in later — a property of having drawn a box, not of the sensors. Parked aircraft going quiet on the ground inflated the same number again. Only gaps that start and end well inside the boundary, with the aircraft airborne at both ends, mean what the statistic claims to mean.

So the decision moved to a criterion drawn from what the system will actually do. Simulated incidents affect an area roughly 20 km across, and the evaluation needs 100 to 150 of them placed. A box is therefore only useful if it holds enough distinct 20 km patches carrying enough simultaneous traffic for an injected effect to have witnesses. That measure did discriminate — from 132 usable sites for the tightest candidate to 556 for the widest. The chosen box offers 263 against a ceiling of 150, enough headroom that no two runs need share a location.

One defect the numbers would never have caught. A shortlisted box started at 5°E, which puts both Amsterdam Schiphol and Brussels — the two busiest fields in the region — just outside it. Its statistics were not alarming; it simply looked like a slightly quieter part of Europe. Moving the western edge to 3°E added 306 aircraft and 64 usable sites for a third more area, the best return in the whole sweep.

The lesson generalises: check a geographic filter against landmarks it ought to contain, not only against aggregate statistics. A statistic cannot report what it never saw.

Longitude is stored first and latitude second, which is the opposite of how people say them and a reliable way to put every aircraft in the wrong place while nothing complains. The 4326 is not decoration either: it is the label saying which model of the earth these numbers refer to, and without it a later question like "everything within 20 kilometres of here" quietly answers in degrees instead — roughly the width of the whole box.

Two clocks, not one

The last line of that payload carries two numbers that look redundant and are not. Position staleness is how old the reported coordinate is. Contact staleness is how long since the aircraft was heard from at all. Most of the time they agree. When they disagree, the disagreement is the signal.

Row 8969 is the whole argument for carrying both. The aircraft is airborne at 10,607 m and was heard from 1.35 seconds ago, so it is emphatically not out of range — yet the position it is reporting is 209 seconds old. That is what navigation degradation looks like in real data. A system carrying only one clock would render this identical to an aircraft that had simply dropped off the network.

Blue marks the last contact, teal the last position update. Both records are from the archive as it stands; the bar is the divergence between the two clocks.

Nothing is silently dropped

Real data is messy. Of the retained reports, 500 have no coordinate at all — the aircraft is being heard perfectly well, some even report an altitude, but there is no position to place them at. Those rows were kept on purpose when the archive was built, so that the path for handling bad records has real input rather than invented input.

A record with no position cannot become an observation. The important thing is what happens instead: it is written to a separate table along with the field that failed and why, the run continues, and the counts of both halves are reported. An ingest that stops at the first bad row is unusable against real data; an ingest that quietly discards bad rows is worse, because the loss is invisible.

The counts were identical every time, so this is the same work taking less time — the reason is below, and it is not a happy one.

What these two columns do not say. It is tempting to read them as "storing the data costs six times what reading and cleaning it costs", and an earlier version of this page said exactly that. It should not have.

The two figures differ in more than one respect. The older one was measured on the machine directly, holding results in memory; the newer one runs inside a container against a database under processor emulation. Storing is almost certainly the bulk of the difference — the emulated component is the database — but that is an inference, not a measurement. Isolating it would need the same code run the same way with only the storage swapped, which the command cannot currently do.

The total is measured. The attribution is not. Worth separating, because a number with a story attached is much easier to repeat than a number on its own.

The number itself moved, and an earlier version of this page said fourteen.

Three runs of the identical command, over the identical archive, on the same machine, gave about ten seconds where two runs a few days earlier gave about fourteen. Every count matched exactly, so there is no question of it being different work.

The difference that can be named is what surrounded the command. The slow figures were taken shortly after the database was started, writing into an empty store; the fast ones against a database that had been running a while and already held a previous run's hundred and thirty thousand records. The obvious expectation runs the other way — writing into a fuller store should be slower, not faster — which points at the database container rather than at the data. It runs under processor emulation on this machine, and emulation is at its worst on work it has not done before.

That is a plausible mechanism rather than a measurement, and it is recorded as one. What can be said without qualification is that this figure moves by about forty per cent with the state of the machine, where repeated runs in a single sitting move by about five. A number quoted without its conditions hides that — which is exactly how the fourteen came to be printed here as though it were a property of the system.

The old number could not be checked, which is why this work existed

The ≈2.4 s had been taken months earlier with a throwaway script that no longer existed. It also disagreed with itself: one project document recorded 2.44 seconds and two others recorded 2.46. Nobody could say which was right, because there was no way to run it again.

A measurement whose method is not written down is not a measurement — it is a memory. The point of this piece of work was less the command than the fact that anyone with the archive can now produce the figure again and get the same answer.

Four things reproduced independently on the way through, having been worked out from the raw file months ago and never checked end to end since: 30,276 observations carrying no altitude, 948 distinct aircraft, every location stored against the right model of the earth, and zero rows anywhere mentioning a real aircraft identifier.

Why twelve batches and not one

The hour is replayed in twelve five-minute pieces, and each piece is stored as a single all-or-nothing act. The alternative was one enormous act covering the whole hour, which was rejected: it would hold a 133,000-row write open for the entire run.

The size of the piece is adjustable, so it was worth checking what it costs. There is an obvious inefficiency to worry about: reading a five-minute slice out of the archive means scanning the whole file and keeping the part that falls in range, so twelve slices means reading the file twelve times.

Eleven extra reads of the whole archive cost nothing measurable. One pass costs about a sixth of a second, and the work done on each individual report dwarfs it — until sixty pieces, where roughly nine of the twenty-three seconds are re-reading and the waste finally shows.

Worth recording because the intuition points the wrong way. The redundant reading is real, visible in the code, and the obvious fix — read once, hand out the slices — would have bought nothing at the sizes actually in use, while adding a new thing to get wrong.

All four of those were measured in one sitting on the cold machine described above, so each is roughly forty per cent above what the same command produces warm. The comparison between them survives that; the absolute values do not. It is the one useful property of measuring a set of options together rather than one at a time.

The consequence is worth being explicit about. If a replay is interrupted, the batches already written stay written — the run is not undone. That is a deliberate weakening of the all-or-nothing promise, made at the level of the run rather than the batch, and it is recorded in the specification and held in place by a test. Anyone reading a failed replay must not assume it wrote nothing.

So which of the two are you looking at?

Until this week, there was no way to tell. A run that stopped after seven batches and a run that completed all twelve left records that looked identical — same kind of row, same start time, and nothing anywhere saying which had happened. Nothing read those records yet, so nobody had been misled. It becomes wrong the moment the evaluation starts counting runs, or a detector reads a stretch of time out of a half-written one and sees a gap in the data as a gap in the sky.

A run now carries a completion stamp, written only once the final batch has landed. Blank means not known to have finished.

The six batches the failed run wrote are still there, and stay there. What changed is that the run itself no longer claims to have finished.

The obvious design was a status with three values — running, failed, finished — and it was rejected because it cannot be honest. A process that is killed outright cannot record its own death. "Failed" would be correct for the failures the program catches and silently wrong for every other kind, which is the worst sort of field: one that looks authoritative and is not. A single blank collapses running, failed and killed into the one thing a later reader can actually assert. That gives up a real distinction, deliberately, because a field that is right most of the time is worse than an absence that is right always.

The other tempting move was to delete a run when it fails, so nothing misleading is left behind. Also rejected: those records are the evidence of what went wrong, and erasing a hundred and thirty thousand of them is a startling thing for a failure to do on its own initiative.

The stamp paid for itself immediately, in a way nobody planned. The gap between a run's start and its completion is now recorded by the database's own clock — and for the verified run it read 9.765 s against the 9.75 s the command reported about itself. It is the first time the replay's timing has been corroborated by a clock the program does not control.

Two faults that only appeared when someone ran it

Both showed up on the very first real invocation, and neither was reachable from the test suite. They share a shape: the place the software runs is not the place the tests describe.

The first was a wrong path — the command looked for its configuration file relative to the project folder, which does not exist inside the container the command actually runs in.

The second is the instructive one. The container is handed the secret from the surrounding environment; when that environment does not have it, the container receives it as an empty value rather than as nothing at all. The code that falls back to reading a configuration file treats "present but empty" as present, so it declined to read the file — and the run failed complaining about a missing secret that was sitting in a file it had mounted.

Every test had either set the value or removed it. None had set it to empty, because no person would. A test suite that enumerates the states a developer thinks of will not cover the state the platform generates.

Real identities never get past the front door

Aircraft broadcasts carry a permanent transponder address and a flight callsign. The licence governing this data requires those to be de-identified for any publication or disclosure, and forbids passing the data outside the institution. The thesis, its screenshots, and — critically — any evidence sent to a hosted AI provider are all disclosure.

So identifiers are replaced at the front door, in one place, rather than in each translator. A new source inherits the behaviour without its author needing to know the licence exists. The replacement is deterministic: the same aircraft is the same fake identity everywhere, which is what makes tracking it across an hour still possible.

Replaced
Not masked, not hashed alongside the original — replaced. The canonical record has nowhere to put a real identifier, so it cannot be retained by accident.
Seed absent
The secret that drives the substitution is never committed. A transponder address is a 24-bit space of about 16.7 million values, so anyone holding the seed and the code could reconstruct the entire mapping by brute force in seconds. A committed seed is a published reverse lookup.
Fingerprinted
What is recorded is a fingerprint of the seed, so that changing it is a detectable event without the value ever being written down.
No cache
The mapping is recomputed every time rather than remembered. A lookup table keyed by the real identifier would be precisely the reverse mapping the rule exists to prevent.

There is a consequence in the set-aside table too. A record that failed validation is stored with the field that failed and the reason — but not the row itself. Keeping the row would be useful for debugging, and would put back into the database exactly the identifier all of the above exists to keep out.

Two rules the database itself enforces

A confidence value can only ever be Low, Medium or High. This is a project-wide rule — no percentages, no decimal scores — and it is written into the database as a constraint, so the store physically cannot hold a value the interface would then have to invent a way to display.

Every hypothesis must record which generator produced it: the deterministic template, a local model, or the hosted one. Recording which, rather than merely that one did, is what keeps the eventual comparison a query rather than a reconstruction — and makes any fallback visible in the data instead of silently absorbed.

The geography question

Locations are stored as flat geometry and converted to true earth-surface coordinates at the moment a distance is asked for, rather than stored that way. The reason is narrow: the one query that justifies using a geographic database at all — find every aircraft dropout within 20 km of a radio anomaly — needs real distance-on-a-sphere arithmetic, and a conversion at query time supplies that while keeping the far larger set of flat-geometry tools available for the clustering work still to come.

The schema also refuses to build at all if the geographic extension is missing, naming it in the error. Without that check the tables build happily, the service reports healthy, and the first spatial query fails much later with a message pointing nowhere near the cause.

Asking the store a question

There is now one address the dashboard can ask for everything a screen needs. It answers with six sections — the scenario, the aircraft, the hypotheses, the detection summary, the radio sensors, the timeline. Two of those six are real. The other four are empty, and the interesting decision was what to put in them.

The four empty ones had no machinery behind them at the time: no detector had been written, no radio sensors existed, no hypothesis had ever been generated. The two solid ones came out of the database that the replay filled.

Three of the four have since been filled, and the fourth — the explanations — is stored and still not served. How long that took to notice, and why nothing could have noticed it, is Nothing it computed could be seen in it, below.

The temptation, and why it was refused

There was an easy alternative. The dashboard already has a set of realistic example values — a station reporting interference, a hypothesis under review, an anomaly score — and the endpoint could have served those for the four sections that have no source yet. The screen would look finished. Screenshots would look impressive.

It was refused, and the reason is the whole project in one sentence: invented data flowing through a real address into a real screen is indistinguishable from the genuine article at exactly the moment it matters. A system whose argument is that machine-generated claims must be checked and grounded before an analyst sees them cannot make an exception for its own placeholder content.

Blank, not "Low"

The same question arrives in a sharper form for the detection summary, which is a single reading rather than a list. An empty list is obviously empty. A single value has to say something.

"Low" is a claim about the world. A blank is a claim about the system. At the time only the second could be made honestly, so the screen showed a dash — the same reasoning that left a half-finished run's completion stamp blank rather than guessing at it.

Updated since. Now that something does assess an hour, "Low" is an honest reading of one that was assessed and found quiet, and the blank has moved: it is the whole summary that is absent when nothing has looked, rather than a dash inside one. The dash turned out to be the weakness rather than the fix — a reader cannot tell a dash meaning nothing to report from one meaning nobody looked — so the screen now says which in words. The system was breaking its own rule, and the compiler found it

A rule written near the start of this project says that no confidence, no severity and no score is ever shown as a percentage or a decimal.

The reason is not stylistic. A hypothesis presented as "87% confident" invites a reader to treat a generated number as a measurement, and the entire argument of this system is that it supports a person's judgement rather than replacing it. Everything is Low, Medium or High.

The dashboard had been rendering 74.2% and 0.92 since the day it was first drawn. Nobody had noticed, and no test had ever looked.

It surfaced while building this endpoint, because the endpoint's job includes locking the two halves of the payload together — and the moment before you lock a shape is the last cheap moment to change it. Changing the values from numbers to bands produced a compiler error naming the exact line: a request to round a number to two decimal places, applied to something that is no longer a number. The machine found the violation of a rule it had never been told about, because the rule had finally been written into a place the machine could read.

One shape, declared twice

The screen and the server each describe this payload independently — the screen in the language it is written in, the server in its own. Nothing checks one against the other, and the failure is quiet: a field renamed on one side leaves a panel silently blank on the other, with no error anywhere.

Which run, when there is more than one

The archive has been replayed several times, so the database holds several runs. The obvious choice is the most recent one — and it is wrong, because the most recent run may be the one that died half-way through. This endpoint asks for the most recent run known to have finished, and only falls back to the newest when nothing has ever finished.

That is the completion stamp from the previous section being used one day after it was added, and it is the clearest possible argument for having added it: without it, the honest query cannot even be written.

A plug designed before the socket existed

This is the piece of work the project will be judged on, and it is easy to miss because nothing about it is visible on a screen.

Months before there was a database, the front door had to be told where to send what it produced. Rather than teach it about the database — which did not exist and might have turned out to be a different database entirely — it was given a socket: a fixed, deliberately narrow description of what it may hand over and nothing about what happens next. Two rules were fixed at that moment:

Nothing leaks
Nothing that belongs to a database may appear in the description. No connection, no database-shaped location, no row identifier for the caller to invent. A coordinate crosses as two ordinary numbers.
One handover
The accepted records and the rejected ones go across together, in a single act. Two separate handovers would leave a gap in which the first had happened and the second had not.

A socket with nothing plugged into it proves nothing, so a stand-in was built at the same time: a pretend store that just remembers things in memory. Everything upstream was developed and tested against that. The real question was always whether a socket designed against a pretend store would still fit a real one. That question was written down, with a prediction attached, and left open for months.

Both are still there, and that is the point. Replacing the pretend store with the real one would have left a socket with a single plug — which is just a connection with extra steps. Two things fitting the same description is what makes it a description.

The answer

It fitted, unchanged. Nothing had to be added to the socket to make the real store work. The prediction on record was that if anything broke it would be the handling of geography or the all-or-nothing rule, and that record identity was safe — and no part of it broke.

The honest reading is softer than that sounds, and worth stating plainly because the flattering version would not survive an examiner. Converting a coordinate into something a geographic database understands was the awkward part, but it turned out to sit one level down, inside the new component, without pressing on the socket at all. And the all-or-nothing rule held for a circular reason: it held because it had been specified in advance, and specifying it is what gave the new component somewhere to put it. So the result is not "the design was good enough to survive contact with reality". It is the guarantee was written down early, so the implementation had somewhere to put it — which is a smaller claim, and the true one.

What did go wrong, and was not predicted

Several fields carry a fixed set of allowed words — the source's name, whether data is replayed or generated. Handed to the database carelessly, one of those writes itself as the internal programming label rather than the word it stands for: the row ends up saying SourceType.OPENSKY where it should say opensky. Nothing errors. The data is simply wrong in a way that reads as deliberate.

It was caught by a test that reads the stored column back rather than one that puts a record in and takes it out again. A round-trip cannot see a fault that is symmetrical — a value mangled identically on the way in and the way out arrives home looking perfect. The same trap is documented one field away in the front door, which is how it was anticipated at all.

The screen stops making things up

Until this week the dashboard displayed a set of realistic example values written by hand. It now asks the system what is actually there, every five seconds, and shows the answer. The example data is still in the code, but its job has changed completely: it is what gets shown while the real answer has not arrived yet, and the screen says so when that is the case.

Why five seconds

The answer takes about four-tenths of a second to arrive. A screen that asked for it more often than that would have several unanswered questions in flight at once, which produces overlapping requests rather than fresher data — the specific way this gets done wrong. Five seconds leaves an order of magnitude of headroom and is still faster than anyone reads a panel. It is a number chosen by hand and written down, not a number tuned.

A rule the machine can check

One rule this project set itself early: everything that reads or writes the database goes through a single component, and nothing else may talk to it. It is the kind of rule that holds for a month and then quietly stops, because breaking it is convenient exactly once and nothing complains.

It is now checked automatically — and the check reads the code as a structure rather than searching it as text. That distinction is not fussiness. A search for a forbidden pattern also matches a comment that describes the forbidden pattern, so a file whose docstring says "never write a query here" fails a search-based check. Faced with that, the rational thing to do is delete the explanation, which leaves the rule enforced and unexplained.

Both halves were tested by deliberately breaking them. A real query added to the screen-serving code turned three separate checks red at once. The identical text written into a comment turned none of them red — while a plain text search over that same file at that same moment found it. The first thing the finished check caught was a line written the day before, in the endpoint from the previous section: not a query, but a piece of type information that meant the file mentioned the database toolkit at all.

First, what a receiver actually reports

The next section describes seven radio receivers, eight channels, neighbours in frequency and neighbours in space. None of that means anything without a picture of what one receiver produces in one moment, so this section builds that picture from nothing. No radio background is assumed.

A receiver is a meter, not an ear
It is not listening to a conversation and it decodes nothing. It measures how much radio energy is arriving and reports that as a number. Think of a sound-level meter rather than a microphone: it tells you how loud, not what was said.
Frequency is the "pitch" of a radio wave
Measured in megahertz (MHz). Different services sit at different pitches, which is how they coexist — the same way two people can talk at once if one is much higher-pitched than the other. Satellite navigation sits at 1575.42 MHz.
A channel is one narrow band of pitch
Rather than report one loudness figure for everything it can hear, the receiver splits the range into eight narrow bands and reports eight numbers, one per band. That is the single design decision the rest of this depends on.

One receiver, one moment. Eight bars, one per slice, each the amount of energy arriving in that narrow band of pitch. This is a quiet moment — the bars are roughly level, which is just the background hiss that is always there. Every receiver produces a picture like this every ten seconds.

Why splitting it into slices is the whole point

If a receiver reported a single loudness figure, the only thing it could ever say is "it is noisy here". With eight, it can say where in the band the noise is — and the shape of that answer is what separates a broken receiver from something genuinely transmitting.

A real transmitter is messy; a broken meter is neat. Anything genuinely radiating spills energy into the slices either side of the one it targets, so the middle chart's pattern — one slice up, both neighbours untouched — is physically odd. It is far more easily explained by the receiver itself reporting a wrong number for one slice. The right-hand pattern, tapering outwards, is what an actual emission looks like.

"Next to" means two different things, and that is the design

The reasoning above asks one question: is this reading isolated, or did its neighbours see it too? An isolated reading points at the instrument; a shared one points at the world. The useful move is that the same question can be asked along a second axis — not the slices next door in pitch, but the receivers next door in distance.

The same shape twice. Solid is the reading under suspicion; outlined are its neighbours; plain grey is everything too far away — in pitch on the left, in kilometres on the right. The left-hand neighbours are fixed by the channel plan and never change. The right-hand ones depend entirely on where the receivers were put, which is why their positions had to be chosen rather than scattered.

 The pitch axisThe distance axis
What is being compared Eight slices on one receiver Seven receivers on one slice
"Next to" means The slice immediately above or below Any receiver within 70 km

Two independent pieces of evidence with the same logic. Agreeing, they make a much stronger case than either alone; disagreeing, they are exactly the kind of ambiguity a human analyst should be shown rather than have resolved for them.

So where does "four below and three above" come from?

Purely from counting. The plan has eight slices and the navigation frequency has to be the centre of one of them, so it takes one of the eight and the remaining seven fall either side — four below, three above. You cannot split seven evenly, and there is nothing to be done about that short of using nine slices.

Which is fine, because the number on each side was never the requirement. The requirement is that there is at least one slice on each side, so the question "did its neighbours see it too?" can be asked in both directions. Put the navigation frequency at the very edge of the plan and it has a neighbour on one side only — the check still runs, still reports success, and is now quietly answering half the question. That is the failure this layout is built to make impossible, and it is why the next section spends its time on rejection rules rather than on the positions themselves.

Giving the invented half somewhere to stand

Half of what this system reads is real: an hour of archived air traffic, replayed. The other half is invented — readings from radio receivers watching the frequency band that satellite navigation uses. Nothing invented has been generated yet, and this piece of work is the reason why. Before you can make up a reading you have to decide who is listening, from where, and to what.

That decision is now made, written down as a file rather than buried in code, and — this is the part that took the work — checked. Seven receivers at fixed positions, each producing the eight-slice picture from the previous section once every ten seconds of simulated time. What follows is about the other axis: where the receivers stand, and why that had to be measured rather than eyeballed.

The seven receivers, at real airfield positions inside the same patch of Europe the replayed air traffic covers. Faint lines join receivers that are within 70 km of each other. Two circles are drawn to show the property the positions were chosen for: from anywhere in this layout, a zone of that size catches some receivers and misses others.

A requirement that could not be checked as written

The specification asked that receivers be positioned "so that a candidate interference zone contains some and excludes others". That reads like a sensible instruction and is, on inspection, not a requirement at all: it names no distance and no threshold, so any set of positions satisfies it. Writing it down changes nothing about what gets built.

It matters because two of the detection rules that come later are meaningless without it. One of them decides "this single receiver is faulty" by checking that its neighbours saw nothing unusual — a receiver with no neighbours has nothing to check, and the rule silently collapses back into "this receiver looks odd", which is what it existed to improve on. The other combines radio noise with aircraft dropouts only when both fall in the same area — and if every receiver is inside every plausible area, that condition is always true and adds nothing.

So the sentence became two rules that point in opposite directions, both checked the moment the file is read rather than whenever a detector eventually runs:

The ruleWhat it forbidsWhat breaks without it
Every receiver has at least one other within the distance A layout spread too thin "Its neighbours saw nothing" has no neighbours to consult
Every receiver has at least one other beyond it A layout packed too tight Every zone contains everything, so location tells you nothing

Together they are stronger than either alone: a layout that passes both necessarily has internal structure, which is exactly what a rule about location needs in order to say anything.

The distance turned out to be a window, not a number

Both rules are stated at a distance, and the obvious move is to pick one that sounds reasonable. Measuring instead showed something more useful: the rules hold across a range of distances and fail outside it, and where that range sits is a fact about the seven positions rather than about the choice.

Below 55.7 kmEindhoven has no neighbour at all — the first rule fails

Seventy was chosen because it sits near the centre of that window rather than near an edge. At fifty-six the property would be true and one small adjustment away from false, with nothing to announce the change. Fourteen kilometres of clearance on each side means an adjustment has to be substantial before it breaks anything — and if it ever does, the layout is rejected the moment it loads instead of quietly producing detections nobody can account for.

An error in our own specification

The channel plan above is the one from the primer: eight slices, the navigation frequency in the fifth. Writing that plan down is where the following turned up.

Our own specification did not add up. Two documents described the plan as "one channel on L1 with four either side". Four either side plus the middle one is nine channels; the plan has eight. The list of frequencies was right and the sentence describing it was wrong — L1 has four below it and three above.

Both documents were corrected, and the point the sentence was reaching for is now enforced directly rather than described: L1 must never sit at either end of the plan, so that "the slices next to it were clean" is a claim about both sides. At the edge it would be half a claim, and nothing would have said so.

The general lesson is small and worth keeping: a sentence restating a table is a second source of truth, and this one drifted from the table it restated before anything had been built from either.

Four tests that were not testing what their names said

An earlier section explains why a test that passes is not automatically evidence. This piece of work produced the sharpest example so far, and the cause was a shortcut taken deliberately.

The routine here is to write tests, watch them fail, and only then write the code — the failure being the proof that the test is attached to something. When the code does not exist at all, though, every test in the file fails for the same uninformative reason: the file cannot even be loaded. The established remedy is a two-minute throwaway version in which every function simply refuses to work, which gives each test its own verdict. That step was skipped, on the reasoning that deliberately breaking the finished code afterwards would catch the same problems.

It did catch them — twenty-five deliberate breakages, four of which changed nothing, meaning four tests had been passing for reasons unrelated to their names:

The testWhy it passed regardless
"Two receivers may not share a name" It duplicated the names of two neighbouring receivers, and the resulting layout was thrown out by the neighbour rule instead. The name rule was never consulted.
"The plan must have eight slices" A different check compared the slice numbering against a fixed count, so it rejected a short plan first and left the count rule unreachable.
"The distance limit includes its own boundary" No pair of receivers sits exactly on the boundary, so whether it counts as inside or outside was never observable.
"An unrecognised setting is rejected" It misspelled an existing setting, which also removes a required one — so it had been proving a rule another test already covered.

Three were repaired in the tests: duplicate the names of two receivers at opposite ends of the layout instead; put a pair exactly on the boundary by deriving the limit from a measured distance. The second was repaired in the code, by making each check test one thing, so that a rule which cannot be seen to fail on its own is not left in the file looking useful.

The finding is that the skipped step is not a weaker version of the same check. Watching each test fail on its own distinguishes "this fails because nothing is built yet" from "this cannot fail" — and all four defects above are in the second group. Two minutes would have shown them. Skipping it cost a twenty-five-way sweep, two rewritten tests and one change to finished code.

The same starting number, the same invented hour

The radio half of this system is invented, and that is deliberate: nothing went wrong over the North Sea on the Monday the archive was recorded, and a system built to explain interference cannot be evaluated on an hour that contains none. But an invented hour is only usable as evidence if it can be produced again — exactly, by anyone, years later. That is what was built this week.

Staging an incident takes invented numbers: how loud the background noise is at each receiver, how it wanders from one ten-second step to the next, which slices of the band it touches. The rule adopted is that a run is determined entirely by three things — which kind of incident, the settings for it, and a single starting number. Feed those three in, get the identical hour out.

What has to be trueWhat it costs if it is not
Same starting number → same hour Without it, the record of what was staged describes something that will never occur again, and no measurement taken from the run can be repeated or checked.
Different starting number → different hour The half everyone forgets. A generator that ignores its starting number passes the first test perfectly — and turns a batch of thirty runs into thirty copies of one run.

Where an invented number comes from decides whether any of this works

There are two ways to produce that noise, and they look equally reasonable written down. The obvious one takes a single random-number generator, starts it from the seed, and draws from it as the grid of receivers and slices is walked through. The other gives each reading an address — this receiver, this slice, this moment — and computes its value from the seed and that address alone.

The left-hand design does not merely permit the problem — it is the problem. The reading in a cell depends on how many values were drawn before it, so reordering the receivers, computing two of them side by side, or adding one more invented quantity per cell later on moves everything downstream. That last case is not hypothetical: the next piece of work adds an injected disturbance to every cell. On the right, each reading is computed from its own address, so nothing can move it — and one reading can be named, recomputed and checked years later without regenerating the hour around it.

Why "run it twice and compare" proves almost nothing

The natural way to test all this is to generate the hour twice inside one program and check the two match. To find out how much that actually proves, the finished code was deliberately broken in four ways, one at a time, and the whole set of twenty-one checks re-run against each.

Read the first and third rows together. A generator that cannot reproduce anything beyond the program run that made it leaves nineteen of twenty-one checks perfectly happy — including every check with the word "identical" in its name, because inside one run of the program it genuinely is identical. A generator that ignores its starting number completely is caught by a single check. Both are catastrophic, and both are invisible to the obvious test.

The two checks that do catch them are the ones the specification named as its failure cases before any code was written: generate the hour in two separate program runs deliberately set up to disagree about internal ordering, and confirm they match; and generate it from two different starting numbers, and confirm they do not. The first of those cannot be done from inside a single program by any means at all. It also carries a guard — each run reports a second number that is expected to differ, so a test setup that quietly failed to take effect is caught rather than reported as a pass.

Then checked somewhere else entirely

Reproducibility inside one machine is the easy half. The same hour was generated on the development laptop and inside the Linux container and reduced to a single fingerprint:

One operating system apart and one version of the language apart. Not one processor family apart — both are 64-bit ARM — which is the honest limit of the claim, and the first thing an automated build on ordinary Intel hardware will settle.

Getting that agreement needed one deliberate concession. Readings are rounded to a hundredth of a decibel before they are recorded, because the mathematical functions underneath — the ones that turn an evenly spread number into a naturally distributed one — are supplied by the operating system, and their very last digit belongs to the operating system rather than to the arithmetic. Rounding puts that difference below the number anyone ever sees. A hundredth of a decibel is far below anything the detection rules will look at, so nothing observable is given up — and the reproducibility claim needs no footnote about which computer it was made on.

The starting number is written down even when nobody chose one. A run started without one draws its own and records it before a single reading is produced, because a run whose starting number was never written down cannot be repeated by anybody, including the people who ran it.

Writing the test for that turned up a limit nobody had noticed: the column that stores the number holds values up to about 2.1 billion, and a number drawn from a wider range would have inserted successfully most of the time and failed unpredictably somewhere inside a batch of thirty runs, hours after it was chosen. The draw is now bounded to fit.

Cost, since every design decision here trades something: computing a value from its address is more work than advancing a random-number generator by one step. A full hour of the seven receivers across all eight slices is 20,160 readings, produced in about a tenth of a second — so a batch of thirty runs generates in under seven seconds, against roughly ten seconds to replay the real hour once.

Staging something wrong

A reproducible quiet hour is the floor to build on, not the point. The point is a run in which something has gone wrong, in a way somebody wrote down beforehand — because only then does "the system found it" become a measurement rather than an anecdote.

There are five kinds of run, fixed, and four of them stage something. Two act on the invented radio grid, one acts on the replayed aircraft reports, one acts on both, and one deliberately does nothing at all.

The quiet control earns its place by doing nothing. It is what the false-alarm rate is measured against, so a run that staged even a little would quietly make the whole system look better than it is. And the faulty receiver stages nothing on the aircraft side on purpose: a broken radio says nothing about the aircraft it happens to be listening to, and a run that moved both would stop being a single-cause case with a single right answer.

Where an incident sits, and who can see it

The system picks a receiver from the run's starting number and reaches out 60 km from it. That distance is not arbitrary: the receiver positions were checked, when they were chosen, to work over a measured band from 55.7 to 85.7 km — wide enough that a zone reaches some receivers and not others. If every receiver could hear every incident, "the neighbours stayed clean" would never be true of anything and two of the detection rules would have nothing to distinguish.

Drawn from the real positions and the real distance. This is the zone one particular starting number produces: centred on Rotterdam, reaching Schiphol 45 km away, and stopping short of Lelystad at 93 km and Eindhoven at 86 km. Change the starting number and the zone moves.

An earlier version of this page overstated what that buys. There are seven receivers, so a batch of twenty to thirty runs has at most seven places to stand — measured over thirty derived numbers, every receiver is used, between two and six times each. What makes those runs different from each other is the combination: the place, the stretch of recorded traffic each one replays, which aircraft happen to be there, and the noise. Not thirty different places.

The incident adds; it does not disturb

The reproducibility work has a payoff here that is easy to miss. Because every reading is computed from its own address rather than drawn in sequence, staging an incident changes the readings inside it and provably nothing else. Take one fifteen-minute run and compare it, reading by reading, against the quiet version of the same run:

readings in the run 5,040 changed by the incident 60 ← 2 receivers × 1 slice × 30 steps byte-identical to the quiet run 4,980 one changed reading Schiphol, navigation slice, 12:02:00 quiet −94.77 dBm → staged −86.32 dBm

Sixty is exactly what the answer key says it should be: two receivers, one slice of the band, thirty ten-second steps. Under the other design — values drawn in sequence — adding the incident would have shifted every reading after the first one it touched, and the answer key written the day before would have stopped describing the data.

A staged reading is labelled exactly like a quiet one. It would be very convenient to mark the affected readings as "interference" — and it would put the answer key on every record, in a field the detection rules and the analyst can both read. The system would then be measured on its ability to read a label it wrote itself. Every reading says the same thing about what kind of signal it is, and a test enforces it.

Two ways for an aircraft to go wrong, and why they must not look alike

The aircraft side is different in kind: these are real recorded reports, and staging an incident means editing them. Two of the runs do, and the difference between them is exactly the difference between the two clocks described earlier.

Both are "something is wrong with this aircraft" and they have different causes. A coverage gap is a receiving problem — nothing arrives, and both clocks run on together. A navigation problem is the opposite shape: the aircraft is transmitting perfectly, so contact stays fresh, while the position it reports gets steadily older. A system carrying one clock would render these identical, which is why the ingest layer was built to carry two.

The staged gap never lands on a track that already has one of its own — that is what the exclusion list described earlier is for, and it is consulted before the choice is made rather than checked afterwards.

What a run is actually made of

"Normal baseline" sounds like an empty file, and it is not. It is the single most carefully constructed of the five kinds of run, because everything the system ever claims to have found is a claim about a departure from it.

So is the quiet run just noise?

Half of it is, and the half that is not matters more. A run has two streams in it, always, and the quiet run has both:

Real aircraft, replayed unaltered
An hour of genuine recorded air traffic — 133,134 positioned reports from 948 aircraft, arriving every ten seconds. In a quiet run not one byte of it is touched. This is not background: it is most of the data, it is real, and it contains all the ordinary messiness real data has.
Invented radio readings, at a noise floor
Seven receivers, eight slices of spectrum each, one reading every ten seconds. Every reading is a nominal power level of −95 dBm with a small seeded wobble around it, typically under two decibels. Nothing is transmitting; this is what a receiver reports when it is hearing nothing but the universe.

So: yes, the radio half of a quiet run is noise — but it is specific, reproducible noise, and it is doing three jobs at once. It is the reference the detection layer will average over to learn what normal looks like per receiver and per slice. It is the false-alarm control: any detection at all during a quiet run is by definition wrong, and that is the only way a false-alarm rate can be measured. And it is the substrate the four other kinds of run add to — which is why it matters enormously that adding an incident changes only the readings inside it.

The shaded patches are the entire incident. Everything else in the right-hand run is the left-hand run, reading for reading. That is a strong property and an unusual one: it means the answer key is exactly the difference between two runs, so nobody has to trust a description of what was staged — it can be recomputed.

One act, not twelve

The replayed hour is stored in twelve separate all-or-nothing acts, and an earlier section explains why: a failure part-way leaves the batches already written in place, rather than discarding an hour's work. The radio grid does the opposite. It is stored in a single act, and a failure leaves nothing at all of it behind.

An earlier version of this page said that of a whole run, and that was too strong. A run turned out to have two halves — this grid, and a stretch of replayed aircraft reports — and each is stored in one act of its own rather than the two being combined into one. So a run can fail between them and leave the radio half behind.

Nothing is deleted when that happens, deliberately: those readings are evidence about what went wrong. What says so is the completion stamp from an earlier section, which is written only after both halves have landed. A half-written run is therefore a run with a blank stamp, and everything that reads runs already ignores those. Making the two halves genuinely inseparable would mean a third way into the front door, bypassing both of the two that exist — a large change to buy a guarantee the blank stamp already provides.

The argument that chose twelve reverses here. What made the replay's partial batches worth keeping was the cost of getting them back; an invented run costs almost nothing to make again, so there is nothing to protect. What decides it instead is the batch that will soon run thirty of these unattended: it has to be able to record a failed run and move on, and half a stored grid is not an absence — it is a stretch of time where a receiver reported nothing, which is precisely the shape of one of the five incidents the system is meant to detect.

Twenty runs from one number

Everything above describes one run. The evaluation needs twenty to thirty of each kind, and it needs them to be produced without anybody sitting there — and reproducible years later from a single integer. That is now one command, and it has been run for real.

One number goes in. From it, each run gets its own starting number, and from that its own zone, its own aircraft, its own noise — and, added late, its own stretch of the recorded hour. That last one was not in the original plan and is the more interesting decision.

Twenty successes that were twenty failures

The first working batch reported twenty runs completed, and every one of them was worthless.

It was run with two-minute runs to keep the test quick, while the setting that says how far into a run its incident begins was left at its default of two minutes. So every incident began exactly when its run ended. Nothing was suppressed, no noise floor was raised, and all twenty runs came out byte-identical to quiet controls — while their records claimed a scenario type and their answer keys named nothing. The command printed ok twenty times.

The specification already forbade the opposite mistake: a control run that stages anything at all fails, because the false-alarm rate depends on it being genuinely clean. The mirror image was not written down, and it is the worse of the two. A contaminated control inflates the false-alarm rate, which is visible in the results. An empty incident inflates the miss rate against something that never happened — which looks exactly like a detector that does not work, and there is nothing in the data to say otherwise.

A batch configured that way is now refused before a single run opens, with a message saying why. Every line of that code was already covered by tests that passed.

And one that had nothing to do with the code at all

The very first attempt did not get that far: it stopped on a missing software library. The batch runs in its own container, built from the same folder as the rest of the backend but separately — and that image had last been built before the database library became a dependency. Every other command in the project refreshes its image first. This one had never worked, so it had never needed to, and nobody had noticed the gap.

Both faults are the same lesson from different directions, and it is the one this project keeps relearning: a test asserts what somebody thought to assert; running the thing asserts what the thing does.

The control room, and what every dial on it does

A batch used to be a command typed into a terminal. It is now a screen, and this section walks through it control by control, because it is the one place where every decision described above becomes something a person actually touches.

The screen as it stands, reading the real store. The twenty runs listed were produced by pressing the button above them, and the list is asked for afresh every five seconds, so rows appear as each run finishes. Nothing here is an example: before the first answer arrives the table is empty rather than populated with something plausible.

The five on the left are a choice of what to stage

Picking one decides what the batch will inject. Four of them stage something; the first deliberately does not, and is the control everything else is measured against. Nothing else on the screen changes when the choice changes, and that is worth knowing, because it makes the selection look inert when it is not — the chosen type is sent with every other setting when the batch starts.

One number, and twenty runs out of it

Base seed is the single most important field and the easiest to misread. It is not one run's number: it is the number for the whole batch. From it the system derives one seed per run, and from each of those comes that run's incident location, which aircraft are caught, and every individual noise reading. One integer therefore reproduces twenty runs exactly, and each run also records its own derived number so any single one can be rebuilt on its own without repeating the other nineteen.

Leaving it blank does not mean "random and lost". The system draws one, records it, and shows it back: Batch accepted — 20 runs from base seed 1817692939. A batch whose number nobody was told is a batch nobody can repeat, so the screen never lets that happen.

What the other seven fields do

Runs in batch must be between 20 and 30. Fewer cannot carry a rate worth quoting; more is only limited by the cost of scoring them by hand. Run duration is how many seconds of simulated time each run covers. Incident offset and incident duration place the staged event inside that window — the default leaves two minutes of quiet before it and several after, so a detector has a before and an after rather than only a during.

Zone radius is how far the incident reaches from the receiver at its centre. Noise elevation is how far the noise floor rises inside that zone. Spectral extent chooses between one slice of the band and the whole of it.

Each row is one run: what was staged, the number that reproduces it, when it began, and whether it is known to have finished. That last column has only two honest states, because a row cannot distinguish a run still going from one that died — both simply lack a completion stamp.

Reset and Replay are visibly disabled, for two different reasons. Reset has nothing to reset: a run is a permanent record, nothing is ever deleted, and until a batch can be interrupted there is no state for it to clear. Replay cannot be expressed by the machinery behind the button — starting a batch means starting at least twenty runs, so "run this one row again" is not a request the system can currently make. Both are future work, and both say so rather than failing quietly when pressed.

One failure the screen currently cannot show you, found by using it.

Starting a batch answers immediately — the work takes about a minute, so the screen cannot wait for it — and the runs then appear in the table as they finish. The first time the button was pressed against the real system, the batch died instantly on a missing piece of configuration and the screen said it had been accepted. The reason existed only in a log file nobody was looking at.

The cause is fixed. The deeper problem is not: because the answer is sent before the work begins, nothing currently carries a failure back to the person who asked for it. A batch that dies still looks like a batch that was accepted and is simply taking a while. That is the same class of dishonesty this project exists to argue against — a system reporting success over a failure — and it is recorded as owed work rather than presented as finished.

Why these five, and how a run gets marked

The five kinds of run are not a list of things that can go wrong. They are chosen so that the system has to tell them apart — and the ways they can be confused with each other are the whole experiment.

Three deterministic rules will read the data. One looks for a raised noise floor co-located with aircraft trouble and calls it possible interference. One looks for aircraft trouble with no co-located radio anomaly and calls it a data-source problem. One looks for a single receiver behaving oddly while its neighbours are fine and calls it a faulty receiver. Each kind of run is built to land squarely on one of those — except the last, which is built to land between two of them.

Marking the other half: there is no right sentence

The second half of the system takes a package of findings and asks a language model to explain them. That output cannot be marked by comparison, because there is no single correct paragraph — two good explanations of the same evidence may share no wording at all, and a wrong one can be word-perfect. So it is marked three times over, by three different kinds of check.

The first two gates are automatic and the third is not, and that is the honest part. A machine can check that an explanation has the required parts and that every factual claim in it traces back to a piece of evidence it was given — and how often that second check catches something is itself a headline result, reported per generator rather than quietly fixed up. But whether an explanation would actually help somebody under time pressure is a judgement, so it is made by two people who do not know which generator produced what, on a pre-registered scale, with their level of agreement reported alongside the scores.

The comparison being run is between a plain template generator — which fills in a fixed form from the findings and cannot say anything that is not in them — and a hosted language model constrained by the same schema and the same grounding check. Both see identical evidence, which is only possible because the runs that produced it are reproducible from one number. If the two arms saw even slightly different data, the study would be measuring scenario variation and reporting it as a difference between generators.

Why the ambiguous run is the one to watch. On the four clear-cut kinds of run, a good explanation and a correct detection mostly agree, and a fluent generator will look strong. On the ambiguous run the evidence genuinely supports two explanations, and the right answer includes saying so. Two of the five scoring dimensions — whether uncertainty is stated honestly, and whether real competing explanations are offered — are aimed squarely at it.

What normal looks like, and noticing when it is not

This is the first thing the system has ever built that forms an opinion. Everything before it moved data faithfully from one place to another and said nothing at all about it.

The order matters and is not obvious. You cannot say a reading is abnormal until you can say what normal is, so the first piece of work was not a rule but a measurement: across the twenty quiet runs already in the store, what do the receivers actually report when nothing is wrong?

The quiet data cannot prove the thing it was measured for

That last sentence is the justification for doing fifty-six separate calculations instead of seven. Checking it produced an awkward result, and the honest thing is to print it.

The fifty-six are, on quiet data, indistinguishable from one another. The quietest and the noisiest sit 0.177 dB apart — against a natural wobble of about 1.5 dB, which is to say the difference between them is invisible inside the noise. A lazy version of this work, one that lumped all eight channels of a receiver together, would produce almost exactly the same numbers.

So no test built from quiet data can tell the careful version from the lazy one. They agree everywhere the quiet data goes. The separation only matters once something has actually gone wrong on one channel and not its neighbours — which is the situation the whole arrangement exists for, and which quiet data by definition never contains.

A requirement can be correct, important, and impossible to demonstrate against the data the system normally produces.

The checks for this one are therefore built from deliberately staged readings, with one channel made loud, rather than from the real quiet hour. That is a slightly uncomfortable position — the evidence for a design decision comes from invented data — and it is better stated than glossed over. It was found by measuring first and writing the checks afterwards, which is the opposite of the order that has caught this class of mistake here before.

The first rule

With normal established, the first rule is the simple one: a reading further from normal than a set amount is a deviation. The amount is a setting rather than a number buried in the rule, so it can be tuned without rewriting anything, and a reading sitting exactly on the line has not crossed it.

What is not simple is the unit of the answer. A single run produces 5,040 readings, and one burst of interference lasting a few minutes covers dozens of them. Reporting each one separately would describe one event dozens of times over and hand the later stages a mess to tidy up. So the rule reports episodes: a continuous stretch of a single receiver-channel reading too high, from the first such reading to the last.

The gap between the two is what makes them two. A normal reading between the fourth and the fifth loud one ends the first episode, so two separate disturbances are never merged into a single finding claiming to have lasted through the calm between them.

Every finding carries how bad it is as Low, Medium or High — never a decimal, never a percentage, which is a rule that holds everywhere in this project. The measured size of the deviation is kept with the finding so anyone can check the reasoning, but it is not what an analyst is shown, because a number like 0.87 implies a precision the method does not have. Each finding also names the exact readings that triggered it, by receiver, channel and moment, so a person can go back and look at them.

The obvious way to write this rule is wrong, and nothing in this project would ever have caught it.

Write it as "flag any reading far enough from normal" and it reads as correct. But it fires just as happily when a channel goes unusually quiet — and a receiver reporting far less signal than normal is not interference at all. It is most likely a receiver that has stopped hearing properly, which is a different problem with a different answer.

Every staged scenario pushes the reading the same way. Interference raises the noise; nothing in the system lowers it. So the wrong version and the right version agree on every run this project can generate, and would have agreed all the way through the evaluation without ever once disagreeing. The flaw is only reachable with data the project does not produce — which is exactly the data a real deployment would eventually meet.

The rule therefore knows which way each measurement moves: signal strength is watched upwards, signal-to-noise downwards, those being the same event seen from two sides. The wider lesson is worth keeping: a synthetic test set proves the system against the situations it was built to contain, and is silent about the rest.

Saying so when it cannot tell

One more decision, small to write and easy to get wrong. Some receiver-channel combinations may have too few quiet readings behind them to have a trustworthy normal at all. The rule cannot judge those, and the tempting thing is to pass over them quietly.

That would quietly destroy the project's most important control. A run with nothing staged in it is supposed to produce no findings at all, and the rate of false alarms reported later is computed from exactly that. If the rule silently skipped what it could not assess, then "no findings" could equally mean "never looked", and a system with no usable normal anywhere would score a perfect record. So an unassessable combination is reported as unassessed, with the reason attached. A correct all-clear and an absent check are different claims, and a system making the first has to be able to show it is not making the second.

The other half of the picture

The rule above watches the radio. This week a second one was built to watch the aircraft, and it needed two different methods for what sounds like one question — how long since this aircraft's position was last fresh?

Still talking
The aircraft keeps transmitting, but the position it reports stops advancing. The reports arrive on time and say, themselves, how old the position inside them is. This is what navigation interference looks like.
Gone quiet
The reports stop arriving at all. There is no message left to read a number out of, so the gap has to be measured as an absence — the time between one report and the next. This is what a failure of the data source looks like.

A rule with only the first method is blind to aircraft that go quiet; a rule with only the second is blind to aircraft that keep talking while frozen. Both are needed, and which of the two shapes was seen travels with the finding, because telling them apart is exactly the job of the stage after this one.

Nothing is wrong with your eyes.

This is not a defect, and it is not a disappointment. It is the problem the whole project exists to address, appearing in its own data. The opening page of this note says the interesting cases all look alike from a distance; this week that stopped being a figure of speech and became a table.

It also points straight at where the answer has to come from. The information that separates a staged disruption from ordinary patchy coverage is not in any single aircraft's report. It is in where the affected aircraft are relative to each other, and in what the radio was doing in the same place at the same time.

Every dot is the same measurement. One aircraft, position stopped updating, long enough to notice. Read one at a time they are indistinguishable. Read together, the red ones are nine aircraft inside a zone twenty kilometres across — and the receivers covering that same patch are reporting a raised noise floor at the same moment. That coincidence is the evidence, and no rule looking at one aircraft can see it.

Two questions to ask a map

The section above ends on an uncomfortable note: a rule that spots every staged navigation failure and cannot say what caused it. The information that would settle it is not in the timings at all — it is in where things happened. So the system has to be able to ask a map two questions. Until this week it could ask neither, and it had nowhere to keep the answers.

The findings needed somewhere to live first

There has been a table for anomalies in the store since the beginning, and it turned out to be the wrong shape for this. It demands three things of anything written to it: how serious it is, the outline of the area affected, and a link to the evidence package it belongs to. Those are all properties of a conclusion. A finding is a step on the way to one — a single aircraft that stopped reporting for four minutes is not serious, covers no area, and belongs to no package yet.

It also had no column recording which run a row came from, which is a strange gap in a system where every measurement is computed per run. Nobody had noticed, because in eighteen months nothing had ever written a row to it.

So findings got a table of their own: a point, a start, an end, what the finding is about, and room for whatever else the rule that produced it needs to carry. One decision in it is worth spelling out, because it is the opposite of what the rest of the store does.

Writing findings replaces what was there. It does not add to it.

Everywhere else, the rule is that nothing is ever deleted — a run is a record of something that happened, and you do not get to revise history. Findings are different in kind. They are derived: run the same rules over the same data and you get the same findings back. They are not a record of an event, they are the current answer to a question.

If they accumulated instead, then the first time a rule was adjusted, last week's answer would sit in the table alongside this week's, with nothing on screen or in a log to say which was which — and every count computed from them would quietly include both. That is a worse failure than losing them, because it looks like data.

"What is near here?"

The first question. Give it a place, a distance and a stretch of time, and it returns the findings that fall inside all three. It sounds like arithmetic anyone could do, and the reason it is not is the whole justification for running a geographic database rather than storing two numbers per row.

Both marked points are exactly half a degree from the centre, and they are nowhere near equally far away. At this latitude a degree of longitude is about 69 km on the ground and a degree of latitude about 111 — so the same half a degree is 34.3 km going east and 55.6 km going north. Draw a genuine 45 km boundary on a chart ruled in degrees and it comes out as an ellipse. One of these two findings is inside it and the other is not.

A system doing this arithmetic for itself would return plausible answers indefinitely. Nothing would look broken; the boundary would simply be in the wrong place by a different amount in each direction, and by a different amount again at each latitude. Handing the question to the database, which knows the shape of the earth, is what makes the answer mean kilometres.

One more thing had to be decided that nobody had written down: whether a finding that began before the window opened counts as being in it. It does. A disruption that started five minutes ago and is still going is exactly the kind the later rules are looking for, and requiring findings to sit wholly inside the window would have quietly discarded the longest ones.

"Which of these belong together?"

The second question, and the one that matters. A single aircraft going quiet is not evidence — a perfectly ordinary hour contains around 138 such events. A dozen of them inside a few kilometres of each other, in the same few minutes, is not an ordinary hour. Nothing in the system could express the difference until this week.

Grouping is also where the outline of an affected area comes from. A single finding is a dot. Only a group has a shape.

Membership chains. The first is close enough to the second, and the second to the third, so all three are one group — even though the two ends are nearly twice the radius apart. Grow the chain and a group can end up spanning any distance at all.

That is exactly right for interference spreading outwards from a source, and exactly wrong for a line of aircraft strung along a single air corridor — which this region is full of. There is no basis yet for preferring one behaviour over the other, so the behaviour is written down as a known limitation now rather than discovered as a defect later.

Two things the grouping deliberately refuses to do.

It does not throw away a finding that grouped with nothing. The temptation is obvious — a lone event among 138 is noise. But a receiver that goes quiet by itself, with nothing near it and no radio explanation, is the signature of an equipment or network problem rather than of an incident. That is one of the two answers this question is asked for, not the residue left over after the real answer.

It does not invent an area for a group too small to have one. A group of one is a point; a group of two is a line; three or more make a shape with area. It would be easy to pad the small ones out into a plausible blob, and the table that stores conclusions would actually prefer it. But a made-up area is a number with no evidence behind it, shown to an analyst as the ground a disruption covered — and that is precisely the sort of figure that ends up in a screenshot and then in somebody's conclusion.

Twice in two days, we believed the manual over the machine

Both of these questions were specified with a confident statement about the database that turned out to be false, and in both cases the truth took one query to establish.

The first: the written specification for "what is near here" stated that a finding sitting exactly on the boundary counts as inside it, because the documentation says the operator is inclusive. It is not. Measured on two real points, the distance between them is 34,338.94 metres, and asking whether they are within 34,338.94 metres of each other returns no. The specification was corrected to describe what the system actually guarantees — one metre inside is in, one metre outside is out — rather than an edge case no real measurement ever lands on.

The second was worse, because it was self-contradictory. The specification for grouping required the distance to be measured on the curved earth and named the specific function to do it with — in the same sentence. That function cannot measure on the curved earth. It has two forms and neither accepts it; both work in degrees, the very thing the requirement existed to prevent. Asking the database to list the function's forms settled in about a second a question that had been written into a specification as a constraint.

The fix was to convert the findings into a genuine metre-based grid before grouping them, and then to measure what that conversion costs rather than assume it is negligible: across the whole region, at most 2.6 metres of error on a five-kilometre radius. The approach it replaced — measuring in degrees — is wrong by around sixty per cent between one direction and another. Both numbers are recorded so the trade can be checked rather than taken on trust.

The pattern is worth naming, because it is now four for four this month. Every confident claim that turned out to be wrong was a claim about a dependency or about the data — never about this project's own code, where claims are made by reading it. The wrong ones were made by remembering documentation.

The same answer twice, to fifteen decimal places

A promise made early in this project is that a run can be reproduced exactly from the single number it was generated from. That promise gets tested at every new stage, and this is the stage where it very nearly failed.

The test is unglamorous: store three findings, ask for the groups, store the same three findings in a different order, ask again, and check the two answers are identical. The membership was identical. The ordering was identical. The centre point of the group was not:

first pass 5.060000171976006 second pass 5.060000171975998

The difference is eight parts in a thousand million million of a degree — call it a nanometre on the ground, roughly a hundredth of the width of a virus. The cause is ordinary computer arithmetic: adding up a list of numbers in a different order gives a very slightly different total, and the database had been adding the coordinates in whatever order it happened to find the rows in. Re-storing them changed that order.

The obvious response is to allow a tolerance, and it is the wrong one. The requirement was never that the centre be accurate to a nanometre — it is that the same input gives the same answer, and a result that shifts depending on how rows happen to be arranged in storage does not, no matter how small the shift. Rounding the number would have buried the cause under a figure chosen to make a test go green. Instead the database is now told what order to add them in, which makes the answer a property of the question rather than of the filing system.

Then the test itself was checked, since a test that catches a bug once may have been lucky: the fault was reintroduced three times and caught three times out of three.

The week the test turned out to be broken

Everything above this point describes machinery being built. This section describes it being checked, and the checking went badly in a way worth setting out in full — because the sequence of wrong answers is more useful than the right one at the end, and because every figure in it was produced by the system measuring itself.

First: the grouping could not tell a staged hour from a quiet one

The two questions in the previous section exist to turn scattered dropouts into one object worth looking at. So the obvious check is: take an hour where a disruption was deliberately staged, take a matching quiet hour, and see whether the staged one produces a bigger group.

It does not. Across twenty matched pairs, at every distance from five kilometres to sixty, the biggest group in a staged hour is the same size as the biggest group in its own quiet twin — differing in one or two pairs out of twenty. Grouping dropouts by place, on its own, cannot tell the two apart.

Choosing what to compare against turned out to be half the problem.

The obvious comparison is a quiet hour. But the quiet hours in the archive replay a different stretch of the afternoon from the staged ones — different aircraft, different weather of traffic — so any difference between them could be the disruption or could be the hour.

A better comparison was already sitting in the data. The runs that stage a faulty receiver use the same stretch of the afternoon as the staged ones and touch nothing about the aircraft at all. Their dropouts are that hour's ordinary background. Every comparison from here on uses those, and they are the reason the numbers below mean anything.

Second: the answer we jumped to was wrong

The natural conclusion was that the detector is simply not sensitive enough — that it finds too few of the disrupted aircraft for a group to form. Measured against the answer key, it was finding about one in five, which seemed to settle it.

It did not settle it, because "not sensitive enough" is a cause, and nothing had counted where the missing four actually went. Counting them put ninety per cent in a single bucket: the aircraft was on the ground.

That number was too lopsided to trust, so it was checked a second way, one that could not be produced by the same reasoning: what fraction of each missed aircraft's reports show it on the ground? The answer was ninety-two per cent of them, all of their reports — parked for the entire hour, with a typical aircraft reporting eighty-six times.

The system had been staging disruptions on parked aeroplanes.

The code that picks which aircraft to disrupt asks one question — is it inside the affected area — and asks nothing else. An aeroplane sitting at a gate at Schiphol is inside the area, so it was chosen exactly like one flying overhead.

The detector then correctly ignored it, because an aircraft on the ground has stopped reporting for the most ordinary reason there is: somebody switched it off. Its silence is indistinguishable from the thing being looked for.

Both halves were behaving exactly as designed, and the combination guaranteed a failure every time. Fifty-five per cent of every staged disruption was landing somewhere nothing could be detected — and the answer key counted every one of them as something the detector ought to have found.

The fix is one check in one place, and its position matters more than its content: it sits above the point where the answer key is written, so a parked aircraft is never recorded as disrupted in the first place rather than being excused afterwards. Third: the grouping still did not work, and the reason is arithmetic

Before making the repair, a prediction was written down: with the disruptions landing on aircraft that can actually be seen, grouping might start to work. That prediction was recorded in advance precisely so it could not be quietly reinterpreted afterwards. It was wrong.

The hit rate nearly tripled because we aimed at far fewer things, not because we found far more. Roughly sixteen ordinary dropouts happen in any hour whatever we do. The repair removed impossible targets from the tally; it did not add detectable ones. The number the grouping depends on went from 1.2 to 1.4.

Groups of four and five form in an ordinary hour by themselves. One or two extra events cannot produce a group that stands out from that — they simply join whichever ordinary group they land nearest. Two separate problems had been read as one, and only the smaller was solved. That is still worth having: the hit rate is now an honest figure rather than one depressed by a defect, and it had to be honest before anything built on it could mean anything.

So the question was changed instead of the settings

There were two ways forward. The first was to make the signal bigger — disrupt more aircraft per hour, in a tighter area, until the groups stand out. It would probably work, and it was rejected: the setting would then have been chosen by turning it up until the detector passed, and every later measurement of the detector would partly be a measurement of that turning.

The second was to ask the evidence a different question, and it is the one taken. Instead of is there a large group here — judged against a standard the background already meets — ask at this place, are dropouts more frequent during this window than at the same place the rest of the hour?

The change is that the comparison is now entirely internal. Same hour, same patch of sky, so it cannot be confounded by one stretch of the afternoon being busier than another, or by some places always being busier than others. Each hour becomes its own control, twice over — and no threshold has to be calibrated against anything outside it.

What makes it worth building is not what it finds. It is what it stops us saying.

The rule it replaces announces "something is interfering here" whenever a radio problem has any cluster of dropouts near it. On the runs where the truth is a broken receiver — nothing wrong in the sky at all — an ordinary background cluster happens to sit close enough seventy per cent of the time. The system would confidently blame interference for most of its own equipment faults.

The replacement, at thirty kilometres, does that on two of the same twenty.

For a system whose entire claim is that a person can check its reasoning, confidently explaining a broken sensor as enemy action is a far worse failure than quietly missing something. The improvement is in what it gets wrong, not in how much it finds.

How firm is that? Firmer than it was, because the whole experiment was built a second time and checked. A fresh set of sixty runs was generated from a different starting number and — this is the part that matters — scored using the settings the first set had chosen, rather than settings tuned to fit it. Anything scored on the data used to tune it will agree with itself; only the other way round can fail.

It did not fail. Five of the six figures landed inside the range the first set predicted, and the pattern across the three widths held. The sixth came out better than predicted, and that is recorded as a miss rather than quietly accepted — a promise about what counts as agreement is only worth something when the surprise is a pleasant one.

A correction that was tempting, measured, and thrown away

There is a flaw in that comparison. The staged window sits in the middle of the hour, and a gap is only visible when there are reports on both sides of it — so events near the middle are easier to see than events at the edges. Quiet hours score above one on this test for that reason alone.

The standard fix is to divide by the same measurement taken across the rest of the map, which shares the middle-of-the-hour effect and not the disruption. It was built and measured alongside the simple version.

It makes no difference whatsoever — the two versions separate staged hours from quiet ones equally well, because the effect applies about as much to the patch as to everywhere else. The simple version was kept and the flaw written down instead. The more elaborate one would have looked more rigorous while measuring exactly the same thing, which is worth knowing before it ends up in a methods chapter.

What it costs to run the experiment now

One consequence of no longer disrupting parked aircraft: some hours now have nothing eligible to disrupt at all. Ten runs in forty staged nothing, against three in eighty before.

That is the repair working as intended — an hour with no suitable aircraft records that it staged nothing rather than staging on an unsuitable one — but it changes the arithmetic of the experiment. Reaching the planned 150 staged incidents now takes about 200 runs rather than 150. That is roughly thirty-five extra minutes of computer time, and the plan has been updated to say so.

The subtler half matters more. An hour of a staged type that staged nothing is a quiet hour wearing the wrong label, and it has to be removed from every score rather than just from the hit rate — otherwise a detector that correctly reports nothing on it gets marked down for missing something that was never there. The count of removed hours is now required to be printed beside the results, so nobody has to take it on trust.

Three rules, or one decision asked three times

The system has three things it is allowed to tell an analyst: something is interfering here, a receiver is broken, or we are not hearing aircraft properly in this area. These were written as three separate tests, each deciding for itself whether to speak. Reading them together shows they are not three tests at all. They are one decision with three possible answers — and nobody had said what order to ask the questions in.

That omission is not cosmetic. If three rules each decide independently, the one that speaks is whichever happens to run first, which is a property of how the program was typed rather than of the evidence. Worse, two of them can speak at once about the same event, and an analyst is handed two contradictory explanations with nothing to choose between them.

Each step can only be reached by failing the one above it, so exactly one answer comes out, and which one is decided by the evidence rather than by the order the program happens to run in. An event claimed at step two is never offered to step three.

The move that matters is where the aircraft side enters

It enters last, and only at step three. Steps one and two are settled on the radio alone.

That looks like an arbitrary preference until you look at how strong each kind of evidence actually is. A broken receiver produces a very particular picture: one receiver screaming while every receiver around it reports a perfectly ordinary afternoon. Interference in the air does not do that — it washes over several receivers at once, because it is out there rather than in the equipment.

Ordinary variation between receivers is about one and a half decibels. The staged fault is eight. That is more than five times the normal wobble, standing alone among six quiet neighbours — about as unambiguous as a measurement in this system ever gets, and no amount of aircraft evidence could make it clearer.

What the old arrangement was actually doing

Previously the interference rule fired whenever a radio problem had any cluster of aircraft dropouts near it. On the runs where the truth was a broken receiver — nothing wrong in the sky at all — an ordinary background cluster happened to sit close enough fourteen times out of twenty. So the system would have looked at an unmistakable equipment fault, noticed some perfectly normal aircraft dropouts a few dozen kilometres away, and announced interference.

And because the coverage rule was defined never to speak about an event the interference rule had claimed, the wrong answer did not merely get added alongside the right one. It took the event away from the rule that would have got it right.

Ordering the questions does not answer that problem. It deletes it.

Every attempt so far had been aimed at making the aircraft evidence strong enough to tell a broken receiver from real interference — and two of those attempts were rejected in this note already, because both amounted to adjusting the experiment until the detector passed it.

Deciding the equipment fault first means the aircraft evidence never has to make that distinction at all. Its remaining job is to separate interference from nothing, which is a much easier question — and, unlike the one it was being asked before, one its measured strength can actually support.

Step two, and the thing it caught in itself

The broken-receiver test now exists. It asks two questions that have nothing to do with each other, which is what makes the pair of them strong:

Is it alone in space?

Interference is a thing in the world. It has a location, and it reaches whatever is near it. A receiver away from its normal while its close neighbours sit at theirs is describing itself, not the air.

Is it alone in the band?

A real transmitter is never infinitely narrow — its energy spills into the channels either side of the one it sits on. One channel raised between two clean ones is a pattern the physics does not easily produce.

The two are kept as two separate records rather than blended into one score, because they are evidence of different kinds and an analyst needs to see which of them is carrying the argument. A receiver whose whole band rises together is reported with lower confidence, not higher: a wideband transmitter parked next to it would look exactly the same from here.

The number the ordering was waiting on

The previous section put this test first on an assumption: that it would actually claim the hours whose truth is a broken receiver, so that the interference rule never has to argue with them. Marked against the answer key across all two hundred and forty staged hours, it claims sixty out of sixty — the right receiver, in the right window, every time. The assumption is not an assumption any more, and the aircraft-side setting that was held back waiting on it can now be chosen.

The same measurement also showed the test was wrong.

It passed thirty-six tests written for it. A deliberate-sabotage pass — breaking the code on purpose, one change at a time, to check the tests notice — killed eighteen attempts out of twenty. And on the real staged hours it called twenty-one interference events out of sixty a broken receiver: the same misattribution the whole ordering exists to remove, running in the opposite direction.

Both receivers are raised for the whole incident. The staged elevation is eight decibels, but it wobbles reading by reading, and about one reading in six happens to land below the line that says anomalous. So one continuous event arrives as seven separate episodes on each receiver — and the gaps do not line up. At the marked reading, A is 7.5 dB above its normal and B is 5.8 dB above its own. Only A crossed the line. Ask "was B anomalous?" and the answer is no. B is then treated as a quiet neighbour, and A — a receiver in the middle of a real interference event — is reported as a broken box.

Two questions that are not the same question

The mistake was a single sentence, written into the design that morning: a neighbour with no reported anomaly is a neighbour reading normally. It sounds obvious. It is false, and the figure above is why.

"Was this receiver anomalous?" is answered with a line drawn so that crossing it means something. That line says nothing whatsoever about what not crossing it means.

"Was this receiver nominal?" is a different question, and it has to be answered against what that receiver normally reads — not against a line built for somebody else's purpose.

The original written specification for this test had asked for the neighbours' readings. The design note written the morning it was built quietly replaced them with the other rule's conclusions, because that version needed no database and was easier to test. The original wording was right.

The two groups do not overlap. A neighbour the incident never reached sits within a decibel and a half of its own normal; one the incident did reach sits two and a half to six decibels above it. Two decibels lies in the gap. And the whole of the second group sits below the old line — not one of those neighbours would have been called anomalous, which is precisely why asking the wrong question hid them.

Two things this does not settle

The confidence levels are untested

Every staged hour in the corpus puts its interference on a single narrow channel, so every report the test has ever produced comes out at high confidence. The medium and low levels exist, and are exercised by hand-built cases, but nothing real has ever produced one. They must not be quoted as measured until a wideband batch is generated.

The ambiguous hour is not ambiguous any more

The scenario built on purpose to have no clean answer stages its trouble on a single receiver — so this test claims all twenty of them, at high confidence, with no trace of uncertainty in the output. That follows from the design rather than from a fault, but it means that scenario can no longer measure whether the system can say "I am not sure".

The second is recorded here before the evaluation is designed rather than discovered inside it. An ordering that gives one confident answer is what this page argued for two sections ago; this is the bill for it, and it is a real one.

What the aircraft evidence actually contributed

The earlier sections of this page spent a long time on the aircraft side: whether a place is busier with dropouts during an incident than it usually is. With the interference test built, that evidence could finally be counted where it matters — on the zones the system actually reports.

A decision made two versions ago, and much better supported now than when it was made.

This page previously recorded a choice: the interference report would not require the aircraft evidence to agree. Saying plainly what the aircraft side is worth

There is an uncomfortable reading of that figure and it belongs on this page rather than in a footnote. The project set out to fuse two kinds of evidence. On the data as it stands, the aircraft side is absent in three quarters of cases and confirms in about one in nine. What the system does today is report on the radio evidence, with an occasional corroborating note from the sky.

That is not wasted work, and the reason matters: the rate comparison is what proved the earlier approach did not work, it is the measuring stick the coverage test was assessed against, and the reason its contribution is so thin here is the same limitation that stopped the coverage test being built at all. But any claim this project makes about multi-source fusion has to be stated at the size the evidence supports, and this is that size.

A fault that only running the whole thing could show

The interference test passed thirty-eight tests written for it, exercised every line of its code, and survived a deliberate-sabotage pass in which every meaningful change to the code was caught. Then it was run over the corpus and immediately produced a hundred and sixty reports for sixty events.

An incident that reaches two receivers has both of them assessed, and each one names the other as also affected. Reporting one zone per assessment describes the same event twice. Every test written for the rule had handed it a single assessment — which is not what the step above it actually produces. The fix groups the assessments into events before anything is reported, and the corpus then yields sixty-one reports for sixty-one events.

This is the third time in this project that the thing being reported has had to be widened. Individual readings had to become episodes. Episodes had to become one verdict per receiver. Now receivers have had to become one zone per event. The pattern is worth naming: what a rule naturally produces is its evidence, and what is worth reporting is always one level coarser than that. In all three cases the test that would have caught it is the one that feeds a rule exactly what the layer below it really emits — and in all three cases the tests written by hand were tidier than reality.

The hours were made again

Two fixes were finished last time and neither could take effect, because both change how the staged hours are made rather than how they are read. So the whole corpus was thrown away and generated again: three hundred hours instead of two hundred and forty, all the same length, all from one version of the generator.

That is a bigger act than it sounds. Every detection figure this page has ever quoted was measured on the old hours, so regenerating invalidates all of them at once. It was done in one go for exactly that reason — one invalidation, one set of conditions, everything re-measured together rather than a figure at a time.

The first fix: a blip is not an event

The radio rule reports a run of readings sitting past its threshold. Nothing stopped that run being a single reading — and a single reading past a threshold is simply what a noisy measurement does occasionally. Those one-reading blips were being promoted into broken-receiver reports on hours where nothing at all had been staged.

Before choosing how long an event must last, the two populations were measured: runs of readings on a receiver the answer key names, against every other run of readings anywhere in two hundred and forty hours. They did not overlap at all. Every single one of the eighty spurious ones was exactly one reading long, while the real ones ran to a median of twenty seconds and a maximum of two hundred and ten.

Requiring an event to last at least ten seconds — two readings instead of one — removed every one of them. The real catches were untouched: sixty of sixty on both kinds of hour that stage a radio incident. It was not a trade-off between catching less and false-alarming less, which is what these decisions usually are. At that setting nothing measurable was lost.

The second fix: an hour that affects nobody cannot be marked

Once staging was restricted to aircraft actually in flight, the hours started affecting very few of them — a mean of 1.7, and eleven hours in forty affected none at all. An hour where nothing was staged has no answer key, so it is identical to its own control by construction and no test can be scored on it. A quarter of one arm was unusable and it did not look unusable.

The fix puts a floor under it: at least five aircraft per hour, chosen from the hour's own seed. On the regenerated corpus no hour affects nobody, and the aircraft-side signal roughly doubled as a result — the measure of "are dropouts unusually frequent here, right now" went from a typical 3.9 to a typical 9.6, and now clears its bar on fifty-eight of sixty interference hours where it managed twenty-six of forty before.

Eighteen places now carry that note where seven did. It is not two sources agreeing, and it should not be described as though it were.

A test that had been ruled out was tried again, and failed in the worst available way

The third question — are we simply not hearing aircraft here? — was abandoned once. Scoring a place on a staged hour against the same place on its control was a coin flip at every radius tried. With five aircraft affected per hour instead of 1.7, that same scoring won twenty times against seven losses, and the test was reopened.

But that is not the question a detector answers, and the distinction is the whole episode. Scoring an hour against its own control compares two hours that differ in exactly one respect — same starting number, same stretch of the day, same aircraft. That is why it can resolve an effect this small. A real detector never gets the control. It sees one hour, against a bar fixed in advance, and everything the pairing quietly cancelled is now variation it has to live with. Two groups can be reliably ordered within pairs and still sit almost entirely on top of each other across hours.

So the question was put the way a detector faces it. The bar for success was written down first, the setting was chosen on two thirds of the hours, and it was scored once on the third that had never been looked at — because a bar chosen on the same hours it is judged against will always look like confirmation.

One hour, one fixed bar, no control availableResult
Coverage-gap hours correctly caught3 / 20
Agreed minimum before the measurement was taken5 / 20
Quiet hours falsely alarmed0 / 20
Interference hours falsely alarmed6 / 20

Catching three in twenty where five was the agreed minimum is a failure. The last row is what makes it a closure.

The test points the wrong way.

At the only setting that keeps the quiet hours silent, it fires twice as often on interference as on the coverage gaps it exists to find. Both stage a real disturbance to the aircraft picture — one removes reports, the other freezes them — and the measurement cannot tell those apart. Worse, this test sits above the interference test in the declared order, so a wrong answer here does not merely add a wrong finding: it can claim the event before the interference test is ever offered it. Building it would put the one kind of hour the system currently gets right sixty times out of sixty at risk, in order to catch a seventh of a kind nothing looks at today. Whether those places would actually collide was not measured — the hazard is established and its size is not.

Why it fails is worth keeping, because it is the same trap in a new costume. Every place on an hour gets a score, and the hour is judged on its worst. But a place holding one or two findings has a score made mostly of the smoothing term that stops the arithmetic dividing by zero — so taking the worst of many places reliably picks whichever place had the least data. That is noise selection, not detection, and every hour has such a place. The worst score on a perfectly quiet hour and the worst on a staged one reach the same maximum, and their middles differ by four per cent. Insisting on at least four findings in a place suppresses that, and is where the little separation there is appears — by which point a third of hours no longer qualify at all.

One detail decides how much weight this carries. The two thirds used to choose the setting and the third used to judge it agree closely — three in twenty against seven in forty. This is a reliable measurement of a weak effect, not a noisy measurement that more hours would resolve. Running it over more hours of the same corpus cannot change the answer. What would change it is the generator: a coverage gap has to become an event with a place, distinguishable from an hour where aircraft reports merely go stale. That is recorded as future work, and it is declined on cost rather than on merit — it would mean making all the hours a third time and re-measuring every figure in this section.

Positive controls, negative controls, and what each kind of hour is actually for

That result is easier to read with a piece of experimental vocabulary that has been doing silent work throughout this project. Every kind of staged hour here is a control of one sort or another, and the two sorts answer opposite questions.

A positive control is a case where something really is there, and the system is required to find it. It answers: can this thing detect what it claims to detect?

A negative control is a case where the thing is not there, and the system is required to stay quiet. It answers: is this thing capable of being wrong?

The second is the one people skip, and skipping it is how you end up with a machine that scores brilliantly by saying yes to everything. A detector with no negative controls has not been tested; it has been flattered. In this project the operational failure that matters most is not missing an incident — it is inventing one. An analyst told an area is being jammed, when in truth the receivers simply stopped hearing aircraft there, has been actively misled and may act on it. So the negative controls here are not a formality; they test the failure with the highest cost.

There is also a distinction within the negative controls that matters more than it sounds:

A plain negative has nothing happening at all. It proves the system stays quiet in an empty room.

A discriminating negative has something real and disruptive happening — just not the thing being claimed. It proves the system stays quiet while there is something to react to. That is a much stronger guarantee, and an empty room cannot provide it.

Here is how the five kinds of hour divide up.

Kind of hourWhat is staged What the system saysScoreWhat it is for
Quiet baselinenothing at all silence60 / 60 Plain negative. Can the system be wrong in an empty room?
One broken receiverone receiver misbehaving that receiver is broken60 / 60 Positive. The single-source fault
Interferenceseveral receivers together something is interfering60 / 60 Positive. The incident the system exists to report
Mixed evidenceone receiver and five aircraft removed that receiver is broken60 / 60 Positive with a negative built into it. See below
Aircraft coverage gapfive aircraft removed, radio untouched silence60 / 60 Discriminating negative. Something real is happening and no radio claim may be made about it

Three kinds of hour produce a finding; two produce a silence. Every catch rate this page quotes is therefore computed over three kinds, not five — and it says so.

This is why the coverage-gap hours are not wasted, and not a duplicate of the quiet ones. The quiet hours prove the system stays silent when nothing happens. The coverage-gap hours prove it stays silent when something real happens on the aircraft side that no radio test should claim. That is a test for the specific failure of evidence from one kind of sensing leaking into a conclusion about another, and the quiet hours cannot test it, because they contain nothing to leak. These hours were written into the project's scope as a deliberately discriminating case before any detector existed, and they still perform that job perfectly. What they no longer offer is a claim of their own.

The mixed hour is a controlled comparison, and its result is a real one.

At first glance it looks redundant: it produces exactly the same answer as the broken-receiver hour, on all sixty. But compare the two properly. For every shared starting number, the mixed hour and the broken-receiver hour stage the same receiver, the same size of fault, on the same channel, over the same window — and the radio measurements they produce are identical, to the byte, in 60 of 60 pairs. The single difference is that the mixed hour also removes five aircraft.

So the pair differs in exactly one variable, and the answer not changing is the finding, not the absence of one: a simultaneous disturbance to the aircraft picture does not corrupt the radio verdict, sixty times out of sixty. The broken-receiver hours alone cannot show that, because they have no disturbance to be immune to. That is a negative control living inside a positive one, and it is worth keeping and worth reporting as such.

What the mixed hour cannot do yet, and what that costs elsewhere.

It was also meant to be the hour that forces the system to choose — two kinds of evidence pointing two ways, and a declared order deciding which wins. It cannot do that today, because the aircraft half of its evidence is exactly the half the abandoned third test would have read. The radio half arrives; the aircraft half is staged, is real, and reaches no conclusion.

That has a consequence for a figure stated earlier on this page, and it should be read carefully. "No place was ever given two different answers" is true, and it is not evidence that the ordering works — because on every kind of hour, exactly one test ever speaks. The ordering is proved by tests written against it directly, and by deliberately breaking it to confirm those tests notice. But the hours themselves have never once put two claims on the same place and made the order do its job. That is a gap in the evidence for the ordering, not only for the abandoned test, and it is stated separately for that reason.

Two things that had been quietly wrong

A measurement that was measuring the instrument.

One figure got worse in the regeneration: how far the detected centre of an incident sat from where it was actually staged, which moved from nothing at all to forty-five kilometres. That would be serious.

It was the measurement. Interference is staged across two or three receivers, and any of them is a correct answer — but the check compared against whichever one happened to be listed first. The giveaway is the numbers themselves: forty-five kilometres and one hundred and seven are exactly the closest and the typical spacing between receivers in this layout. It had been reporting the distance between two right answers. Asked properly — how far to the nearest staged receiver — it is zero on every single hour.

Worth keeping as a habit: a figure that matches a constant of your apparatus is probably measuring your apparatus.

The database had been reading the whole library to find one book.

Every one of these measurements asks the same kind of question — give me this hour's observations — and the store had never been told to organise itself around which hour a record belongs to. So each question read all twenty million records from the beginning, three hundred times over. A single pass took half an hour.

Nothing was wrong with the answers, which is exactly why it survived every test for months: an unindexed lookup returns the right rows, just slowly. Building the index took one migration and turned a half-hour pass into seconds.

One thing could not be done, because of something never written down.

The ideal regeneration would remake the same hours with the fixes applied, so every before-and-after comparison was the same hour twice. That was impossible: each hour is generated from a number, and the numbers that produced the original batches were printed to the screen at the time and never stored. Exactly one of them survives anywhere, in a note that happened to quote it.

So the new corpus is a new sample, reported as one, and the comparisons on this page are between two sets of hours rather than between the same hours twice. The cost of having recorded those numbers would have been three integers per row. They are recorded now — and every hour can re-derive its own number from what its row says, so the claim is checkable rather than merely stated.

The package the model is allowed to see

Everything up to here produces findings. Nothing up to here produces an account. The piece that bridges them is a package: one event, everything known about it, and — this is the part that took the work — everything not known about it, each absence labelled with which kind of absence it is. It is the only thing the AI model ever receives.

That last sentence is a boundary rather than a convenience. Checking whether a generated explanation cites something real has no practical answer against twenty million stored readings. Against a fixed package of two dozen statements it is a list lookup. The package is what makes the safety check downstream possible at all, so its contents are the safety check's whole horizon.

One event, one package — not one hour

An hour that breaks two receivers is two events, and the ordering built earlier already refuses to merge them. So the package follows the event rather than the hour, and it does not regroup anything by time or place: that grouping exists one step upstream, and a second copy of it would be a second opinion about the same question. What the package does own is the frequency dimension, because nothing before it joins up a disturbance that appears on four neighbouring channels of one receiver. Left alone, that would arrive at the model as four separate events.

Three ways of not knowing something, kept apart on purpose

A package that stays silent about the aircraft side is telling the model something, whether it means to or not. So every absence is labelled, and there are three of them:

The labelWhat it meansWhere it comes up
Not applicableNever asked, by designA single broken receiver never reaches the rule that weighs the aircraft evidence, so there is no aircraft evidence because none was sought
Could not be measuredAsked, and there was no basis to answerA stretch of sky with no reporting gaps at all in the comparison period, so the comparison has nothing on one side of it
Measured, and found nothingLooked at, and it did not rise to anythingA disturbance lasting one reading, seen and deliberately not called an event

Collapsing the first two is the dangerous one, and it is easy to do by accident: leave the aircraft side out of a broken-receiver package and it reads as we tried and could not tell, which claims an uncertainty the system does not have. The most consequential single line any of these packages carries is the second label, because in 41 of 60 reported interference zones the aircraft side could not be measured at all. The project's fusion claim is weaker than the word suggests, and the package is the object that has to say so to the thing writing the explanation.

One more rule, for the same reason in reverse. When the ordering rejects a competing explanation, that rejection is carried in its own compartment and never among the evidence. Two rules disagreeing about an area, filed side by side, reads as two rules agreeing about it — which inverts the truth exactly, and is the error a fluent model makes without being prompted.

Then every citation was looked up, and one in eleven pointed at nothing

Each statement in a package carries a note saying which raw readings it came from. Those notes had been written since the findings were first stored. Nothing had ever read them.

Reading them was expected to be a formality — the notes were already known to describe readings precisely enough to be unambiguous. Over twenty-four real packages they cite 1,021 readings between them, and 91 of those pointed at a moment when nothing was recorded.

What made it diagnosable was that the failures were not scattered. Every radio citation resolved. Every citation from one of the two kinds of aircraft finding resolved. All ninety-one misses were the start of the other kind.

Solid teal is a real stored reading. The dashed stretch is inference. The open red circle is where the citation used to point: the moment the position stopped updating, worked out by subtracting the staleness the report itself declares.

The cause is a distinction the design had quietly collapsed. A finding carries two kinds of time: the window, which is what the rule concluded, and the citation, which is what the rule read. For a gap in reporting those coincide, because the window's ends are the two reports either side of the silence. For a frozen position they cannot coincide. That finding is raised when an aircraft keeps transmitting while its reported position stops advancing, and the rule works out when the freeze began by subtracting the staleness the report declares from the report's own arrival time. That instant is a reconstruction — and it is, by definition, the one moment in the whole window at which nothing was recorded, because a position that has stopped updating is precisely what the finding is about. The citation pointed at the only place a citation can never land.

The fix keeps the window, which is the right answer to the question the window asks, and adds the two reports the rule actually read beside it. Moving the window instead was considered and refused: it would make every such finding shorter than the event it describes, which would quietly change counts elsewhere that depend on it.

Two reasons this is worth more than the bug.

It was invisible to every check that existed. The notes were produced, stored, covered by tests, and survived a deliberate sabotage pass. None of that touches them, because nothing consumed them. A field that is written and never read is untested no matter how green the suite is, and this one sat that way across two complete rebuilds of the data and several hundred simulated hours.

The obvious test would have missed it. Written against a gap-in-reporting finding, a traceability test passes — both of its ends are real rows. Two mechanisms produce the same shape of output, only one of them can satisfy the requirement, and a test built on the wrong one proves nothing about the other while looking thorough. This is the second time on this project that a test could not have failed. The general form is now written down: a check is only as strong as the variety in what it is run against.

The cheaper fix was to stop asking twenty-six times

The same measurement settled a performance question that had been deliberately left open. One lookup costs about 65 milliseconds, because the store can narrow to the right hour and no further and then discards some thirty-eight thousand rows by inspection. Whether that mattered depended on how many citations a package carries, which could not be known until packages existed.

AskingTypical packageWorst seen
One question per citation194.5 ms748 ms
One question for the whole package10.5 ms131.5 ms

A package cites 26 readings at the median and 105 at the worst. Asked one at a time, the thirty-eight thousand rows are inspected once per citation; asked together, once per package. That is roughly 19×, and it is the difference between a design decision and a permanent change to how the data is stored. The obvious response to sixty-five milliseconds a lookup was to add another index — a structural change that has to be migrated, reasoned about and maintained. The cheaper response was to stop issuing the question twenty-six times. The index is still a sensible idea; it is no longer a prerequisite.

What the package now refuses

Three refusals, each of which nearly got written as a shrug:

  • A citation matching nothing. The package would be asserting something it cannot show.
  • A citation matching two things. Worse, and the one most likely to be waved through. The package would carry whichever of the two the store happened to return first, and nothing anywhere would record that the citation was ambiguous. This project has now been caught three times by results whose order nobody specified.
  • A citation in a shape nothing recognises. Refused by name rather than attempted. One of the detection rules produces notes naming an aircraft with no time attached, which as a question to the store matches every report that aircraft ever made — and a lookup assembled from whatever fields happen to be present would take an empty note and match the entire hour.

Not every statement cites a reading, and that had to be handled without leaving a hole. Of 332 statements across those packages, 72 cite nothing at all — a verdict, a neighbouring receiver confirmed normal, a rate comparison. Those are judgements, and there is no stored row for a judgement. But carries no citations must not be something a statement can drift into, or a check that skips them passes by default on anything that merely forgot. So each statement declares which kind it is, and both lies are refused: one claiming to cite a reading while citing nothing, and one calling itself a judgement while carrying citations.

The first explanation, and why it has no AI in it

Everything up to here produces evidence. A package now exists for each event, every statement in it is traceable to a stored reading, and each kind of not-knowing is labelled. What nobody could yet do was read one. This is the stage that turns a package into an account a person can take a decision on — and the first thing built for it is deliberately the one with no model behind it.

One shape, three producers

Three things will eventually write explanations: a mechanical summary with no model, a hosted model, and a local one. The first thing built was none of them. It was the shape all three hand back — a title, a confidence, a summary, the evidence it rests on, what speaks against it or is unknown, what else it could be, and what to ask a human being to do.

Fixing that first buys two things. A screen can show any explanation without knowing which produced it; and the study can compare them at all, which is impossible if the three arms return different things. Doing it afterwards would mean bending two finished generators to fit a third.

The shape is a rule about what a model may emit, not a description of what it did. A hosted model is handed a translated copy of it and is physically unable to produce anything that does not conform. That translation is where a guarantee quietly dies: a confidence restricted to three words on the original, but translated into "any text at all", would pass every test written against the original and lose the restriction at the only boundary that relies on it — because the original never runs during a constrained generation. So every guarantee is checked twice, once on each.

Confidence is three words, and cannot be a number

Not "rejected if it is a number" — unable to be one. The field holds one of three values and 0.87 is not among the things it can contain. This rule has been in the project since the interface work, and it exists because a decimal invites arithmetic nobody justified: a reader who sees 0.87 will compare it with 0.84 as if the gap meant something.

Stated as a limit rather than claimed as a solution: this stops a number appearing in the confidence field. It cannot stop a generator writing "High (about 87% sure)" into the summary. That half is the screen's job, and it is not built.

A citation has to name two things, because one does not identify

The evidence an explanation rests on is a structured reference, never a sentence. A sentence cannot be looked up, so an explanation whose citations are prose does not make the checking step awkward — it makes it impossible.

The reference names the rule and the kind of finding, and it took building this to close a loose end the project had been carrying. Two different statements share one rule name: the rule that notices an aircraft's reporting break, and the comparison that counts how many such breaks happened inside a zone against outside it. By rule name they are identical while making completely different claims. Naming the pair separates them, and the mechanical generator is where that stopped being an argument and became behaviour — grouped by rule alone, the two collapse into one citation making neither claim.

Nothing is allowed to default to empty

This is the same principle the packages already use, one layer up. If a list of alternatives can quietly be empty because a generator forgot it, then weighed the alternatives and found none and never mentioned alternatives arrive looking identical.

One asymmetry on top of that is worth stating, because it is a judgement rather than a rule. An explanation must offer at least one alternative and is allowed to offer no evidence against itself. "Nothing speaks against this" can be true. "Nothing else could explain this" essentially cannot — and a system built to stop a fluent model overclaiming must not accept the second sentence from it.

The producer with no model, and its two jobs

The mechanical generator reads a package and writes the explanation out by rule. Both of its jobs are permanent; it is not scaffolding to be deleted when the model arrives.

The jobWhat it means
The safety net When a model returns something malformed, or claims something the evidence does not contain, the analyst has to receive an explanation rather than an error message.
The yardstick The question this project asks is whether a language model adds anything over an honest mechanical account of the same evidence. That cannot be answered by looking at model output alone, however good it reads. It needs the mechanical account of the identical package, graded blind alongside it.

The first job produces a constraint that is easy to say and easy to break: a fallback that depends on the thing whose failure summons it is not a fallback. If the hosted service is unreachable and the fallback needs to reach the network, there is no fallback. That is checked rather than promised — a test follows every module the generator can reach, however indirectly, and refuses any network client or model library anywhere in that chain.

That check is a search that finds nothing when it passes, which is also what it finds when it is broken. So it is fired once at a part of the system that genuinely does reach the network, to watch it go off. This is the same lesson as the store's safety catch, which passed ten tests of its own and did not fire when it mattered: a guard tested only against the case it is meant to allow has been tested against its author's assumptions.

A baseline must not flatter itself

The second job produces a subtler constraint. A fault in a model's explanation shows up as a bad explanation a human grader can see. A fault in the yardstick shows up nowhere — it moves the measuring stick, and every comparison drawn against it moves by the same amount in silence. Three decisions all push against that.

  • Confidence is inherited, never recalculated. Each package already carries the confidence of the rule that decided it, and that value was measured and argued when the rule was built. Working out a second one here would be the same judgement expressed in two places, and two expressions of one judgement eventually disagree while both keep working. It also means the mechanical account can never be more confident than the rule it is restating.
  • One citation per kind of evidence, not per piece. A real package holds thirty-four pieces of four kinds. An explanation with thirty-four citations is complete and unreadable, and a yardstick nobody can read is not one a grader can mark.
  • The alternatives are what the system itself ruled out. When two rules claim the same event, the losing claim is recorded as a competing account. Those are the alternatives. Inventing plausible-sounding ones while ignoring the ones the system actually set aside would be the explanation disagreeing with its own evidence.

Underneath all three sits a property the whole design leans on: every word of evidence the mechanical generator cites is copied from the package, so it cannot cite something that is not there. That has a consequence worth stating carefully, because the obvious reading is backwards. When the automatic checking step is eventually run over one of these explanations, a pass proves nothing — it was guaranteed before the check ran. A failure would be a fault in the check. It is the one input whose right answer is known in advance, which is exactly what a checker needs and rarely gets.

One trap, avoided, and the obvious repair was worse than the fault. The detection rules write confidence in lower case; the explanation format requires a capital. Passing the value straight through fails. The instinctive fix — capitalise the first letter — turns low into Low correctly and also turns certain into Certain, which is accepted here and rejected two layers downstream, at a point where the package that caused it is no longer to hand. Matching the word against the list of permitted ones instead refuses the unfamiliar word where it can still be explained.

Thirty-seven tests, a deliberate sabotage pass, and then real data found two more

Three separate checks were run on this, and each found what the one before it could not.

Before writing the generator at all, a throwaway stand-in was put in its place — one that returns the same fixed, valid-looking explanation whatever it is given. Eleven tests passed against it. Four of those were genuinely wrong: one asserted that a made-up claim was absent and was satisfied by a generator citing a different made-up claim; another checked that the recommended action tells a human to look at something and was satisfied by the words "Check it." The other seven pass by their nature, and the test file now says which and why rather than leaving a reader to wonder.

Then the sabotage pass: thirty-five deliberate faults introduced one at a time, to see whether the tests notice. Twenty-nine did. The four that slipped through had one shape between them, and it is not that the tests were testing nothing — each was passing for a reason other than the one its name claimed, which no amount of reading would have revealed. A test that a damaged package is refused was satisfied because the sabotaged version also refused it, for a different reason, with a message that happened to contain the same words. A test that an explanation says how many findings it stands for was satisfied because its two examples differed in another number as well. Each repair removed the accidental route to the answer rather than asserting harder.

Then it was run over the forty-one real packages. It produced forty-one explanations, refused none, and every one of the 164 citations was found in the package it came from. It also found two faults in the writing that no test could have:

  • A doubled full stop. One rule's recorded reason is three sentences long and already ends in one, so adding a stop produced two. Every test example ended without punctuation, because I wrote them all. Cosmetic anywhere else; here the output is read blind beside a language model's, where punctuation a model would never produce reads as a difference in quality rather than as a typo.
  • A sentence pointing at the wrong thing. The summary introduced the deciding rule's words as "the rule's own conclusion", which is right for most packages and wrong for the commonest case here — where the rule's recorded remark is a note about what it could not establish. Nothing was invented and nothing was malformed; a statement of ignorance was simply presented as a finding. It now reads "the rule states", which is true of whatever the rule recorded.

Both belong to the same family as the two faults the earlier end-to-end run found: properties of what real data contains, rather than of what the code says, and therefore invisible to any test whose examples the author chose.

Something the study has to declare before it scores anything

Because the mechanical account takes its confidence from the rule rather than forming one, it cannot be badly calibrated in the way a model can — confident about thin evidence, hedged about strong evidence — which is one of the things the grading instrument is designed to catch. On that particular dimension the study is therefore not comparing two answers to one question; it is comparing an answer with a restatement. That is arguably the point, since a model arm that cannot beat a restatement of the rule's own confidence has added nothing there. But it has to be said in advance rather than discovered during the analysis, and the second grader has to be told.

A smaller one came out of the same run. The mechanical account emits exactly four citations on every one of the forty-one packages, because both kinds of package the system currently makes happen to carry four kinds of evidence. Its length does not vary with the event, where a model's will — so any part of the grading that rewards completeness or concision is being marked against a constant for one of the arms.

Two things that only matter because a model is about to be asked

An explanation can now be written for every package, mechanically, with no model involved. The next thing built was not the model. It was the pair of things that are worthless until there is one, and expensive to add once there has been: somewhere to keep what a model says, and a decision about what a person sees when what it says cannot be used.

The table was built too early, and this was the last free moment to fix it

Explanations had a place to live from the very first version of the store — written months before anybody knew what an explanation would contain. Three of its fields turned out to have the wrong names, and two of the seven did not exist at all. An explanation could be produced and read; it could not be kept.

The tempting fix is a translation layer: keep the old names in the store, convert on the way in and out. That is a permanent place for two vocabularies to drift apart quietly, and it makes every later reader learn both. The names were changed instead — which was possible only because the table still held nothing. Renaming a column with rows in it is a migration with risk; renaming an empty one is a rename. That window closes the first time a model answers, and the first version of the store carries a comment saying exactly this about a related field: a column added after a study has run is a study that has to be run again.

Explanations are added and never replaced — the opposite of how findings are kept.

A finding is a conclusion a rule drew from stored readings. Delete it and re-run the rule and you get it back exactly, so the store keeps one current set per rule per hour and overwrites. A model's answer is not like that. It cost money, it was said once, and asking again produces a different thing rather than the same thing. Two answers are two facts.

That is not a philosophical point. One package will legitimately carry both a model's explanation and the mechanical one that replaced it after the model's failed a check, and the pair is the record of what happened. Overwriting would keep the replacement and destroy the evidence of why there was one.

Checked has three answers, not two

An explanation is stored before anything examines whether its claims hold up. So the field recording that examination has to distinguish passed, failed, and nobody has looked yet — and a plain yes/no makes the last two identical. The same mistake, in the same shape, that the evidence packages already had to avoid twice. It is written so that no future code can treat "not checked" as "not passed" by accident.

Two further refusals run in opposite directions, and both are deliberately a second line of defence rather than the first. An explanation claiming to come from a model, but carrying neither the model's name nor its exact words, is refused. So is a mechanical one that names a model, since no model ran and that name would be written into the columns the study's reproducibility rests on. Both are already impossible one layer up. They are kept because this is the one kind of failure nobody can repair afterwards: a study run against a model whose identity was never recorded is a study to run again.

What a person sees when the answer cannot be used

There are two ways a model's answer fails here, and only one of them is the obvious one. It can cite a rule or a reading that is not in the package it was given — which is the failure the whole project exists to catch. Or it can fail to fit the required shape at all.

Either way, the analyst must see neither that answer nor an error message. So the request is made again a fixed number of times, and if it still cannot be used, the mechanical account is produced instead. The ceiling is not politeness. A loop that retries without a limit is the realistic way this project spends money it did not mean to — far more likely than the study itself, which costs pennies.

Two different failures end in the same place, and merging them would be a real mistake.

 The service is unreachableThe service answered badly
What was wrongnothing about the questionthe answer
Right responseask the same question somewhere elseask again here, a fixed number of times, then stop
Wrong responseasking the same place harderasking a different service the same failed question

Both end with the analyst reading something other than the first service's answer, which is exactly what makes them easy to treat as one. They are not. Asking a second model a question the first answered badly is not a recovery; it is a second guess wearing a recovery's clothes. The two are held apart by the type system rather than by discipline, and the tests prove the separation is observed rather than merely intended.

The retry may do nothing at all, and there is already a measurement that will say

The study fixes the model's settings so that the same question always produces the same answer — that is what makes a result reproducible, and it is not negotiable. But a retry asks the identical question. If the service really is that predictable, the identical question returns the identical answer, which fails the identical check. The retry is then a second payment for the same refusal, and the limit's only effect is to multiply the cost of a failure.

Whether a given service behaves that predictably is not something its documentation can tell you. These services process many requests together, and adding the same numbers in a different order gives very slightly different results — the same effect that moved a measured centre by less than a billionth of a metre earlier in this project and broke a reproducibility rule. There is already a small measurement planned, for an unrelated reason, whose question is exactly is this service actually as predictable as it claims? Without changing a line, it also answers whether this retry is a mechanism or a tax.

So the limit is two attempts for now, chosen rather than measured — the only figure in this part of the system with no measurement behind it, and labelled that way where it is written. The reasoning is lopsided rather than confident. Guessing too high costs about twenty extra requests out of a budget of two hundred. Guessing too low throws away an answer a model might have got right on a second attempt, and understates the model in a comparison that cannot be run again.

The sabotage pass found three tests that were not testing what their names said — and one of them was hiding something worse than a weak test.

Thirty-two deliberate faults were introduced one at a time to see whether the tests noticed. Twenty-nine were caught first time; after the three gaps below were closed, all thirty-two were. One of the three was a test that checked each rejected answer is written to the log: it passed with that logging deleted, because a separate summary line happened to mention the same two words. It was rewritten around a case where no summary line is produced.

The third is the one that matters. Nothing had ever asked what happens if the checking software itself has a bug. As written, a crash inside the checker would have been caught and filed as "the model cited something that is not there" — so a defect in the checker would inflate the reported rate at which models make things up, and the model would be blamed for the checker's fault. That rate is a headline result of this project, not a safety net. The behaviour was already correct; nothing proved it, which means nothing would have noticed it changing.

Spending money on purpose

Every request to a hosted AI service costs something. Nothing in this project has made one yet, and the last thing built before the first one is the thing that counts them — because the moment to install a spending guard is before the surprise, not after it.

What it is actually guarding against, which is not the study

The obvious assumption is that the guard exists because the study is expensive. It is not. Measured across the real evidence packages, a whole scored study — twenty packages, one answer each, two producers — comes to roughly 33,000 units of input. At any current price that is single-digit pounds at worst.

The risk is a loop. A retry that never gives up. A batch that regenerates every time somebody refreshes a page. A test accidentally pointed at the real service instead of a stand-in. None of those is a large study; each of them is an unbounded one. So there are two guards, at deliberately different distances:

A run set up with only a money ceiling would have had no guard.

So the request count is required and is the guard; the money ceiling is optional, fires one request late by construction, and says so where it is written rather than in a comment somebody has to find.

The code then made exactly the mistake the plan had just been corrected for.

The first version put both checks in one shared place. That looked like removing a duplication — two limits, one routine that enforces limits. It meant the request count was also evaluated after the request rather than before, so a ceiling of twenty stopped the run on the request that filled the budget rather than the one that would have exceeded it, and only nineteen ever completed. Seven tests caught it within a minute.

The off-by-one is not the interesting part. The argument for keeping those two checks apart had been written down, in the plan, the same morning — and the code merged them anyway, because at the level of where does this line go they look like the same statement about the same object. Worth recording as a general finding rather than an embarrassment: a distinction argued in prose does not survive contact with a tidy-up unless something about the structure resists it. Two separate routines, each stating why it is separate, is the weakest available form of that resistance. The tests are the real one.

"Not priced" and "cost nothing" are different claims

If four requests cost five pence each and two could not be priced, the honest statement is at least twenty pence — not twenty pence. Both are the same arithmetic and only one of them is true.

So unpriced requests are counted separately, left out of the total, and the record says whether the total is complete or a floor. That matters more than bookkeeping tidiness because the actual cost of the study is a reported result of this project, not an internal detail — and a figure that quietly omits what it could not price is worse than no figure. It is the third time this project has had to insist that not measured and measured as zero are different values, after the evidence packages and the record of whether a claim was checked.

Why "stop the run" needed defining at all

Two mechanisms already exist to keep a failure away from the analyst: one tries a different service when the first is unreachable, and one asks again and then falls back to the mechanical account. Both are good. Both would be disastrous here.

If running out of budget looked like an ordinary failed request, neither mechanism would stop the run. They would handle it. The study would quietly finish on mechanically written explanations while every stored record said the answer came from a model — and nothing would look wrong until somebody compared the results against something. Running out of money is therefore deliberately a different kind of error, outside the family that either mechanism is allowed to catch, and the test that proves it wraps a real retry mechanism around a real budget and requires the error to come out the other side untouched.

One limitation, worth stating rather than discovering. The budget is held in memory for the length of a run. A process that dies and is restarted begins counting again from zero. That is a second reason the provider's own cap exists, rather than a belt-and-braces one.

Asking a model, without letting it say anything it likes

This is the part the project is actually about. Everything before it produces evidence and writes an explanation by fixed rules; this is the first thing that hands a package to a model and asks it to reason. It is built, it is tested, and as of this week it has made its first real requests — eight of them, read by hand. What they showed has a section of its own further down; what follows here is how the arrangement works.

The model is not asked to behave — it is prevented from misbehaving

The obvious way to get a structured answer out of a language model is to ask nicely and check afterwards. That is not what happens here. The required shape is handed to the service along with the question, and the model is physically unable to emit anything else: the shape is enforced while the words are being chosen, not inspected once they have been.

So a whole class of failure disappears. The answer cannot be missing a field, cannot invent an extra one, and cannot express confidence as a percentage — the three words are the only things that fit through.

But the constraint covers shape, not quantity — and that correction was earned by building it.

An earlier decision on this page's underlying plan reasoned: since the shape is guaranteed during generation, any answer that fails the check afterwards must mean the service broke a promise it advertises — a rare and alarming event deserving its own line in the log rather than being counted with ordinary problems.

Half of that survives. The constraint mechanism carries structure — every field present, types correct, confidence one of exactly three words — and does not carry rules about how many. "At least one supporting citation." "At least one alternative explanation." "The summary is not blank." None of those can be expressed in the thing doing the constraining.

They are not lost; they move. They are still enforced, by the same shape definition, checked when the answer arrives — as a rejection rather than an impossibility. Which means a rejected answer is no longer automatically alarming: an empty citation list will happen from time to time and is ordinary, while a confidence of "Certain" is still the rare serious event, because that rule genuinely is enforced during generation. The system now tells the two apart and says which it saw. Counting them together would have buried the event worth noticing under the one that is expected — which is exactly what the original decision was trying to prevent, arrived at by a route it did not anticipate.

The instructions are part of the experiment, not a setting

Two producers given different instructions are not two answers to one question. So the instruction text is written once, reviewed before any request is made, and would be shared word-for-word with a second model if one is ever added. Nine binding rules: the package is the only source; every citation must name a rule and a finding type copied exactly from it; the rejected explanations are context and may never be cited as support; confidence is three words; at least one genuine alternative; an absence of measurement is not an absence of contrary evidence; the aircraft names are pseudonyms; the recommended action must be something a person can actually do; and write plainly without padding.

Every stored explanation carries a fingerprint of the exact instruction text that produced it. Instructions drifting part-way through a study is invisible in the output and would quietly ruin the comparison; a fingerprint makes it a thing you can check rather than a thing you have to remember.

Three things are deliberately left out. No glossary of what the anomaly types mean — the package already carries each rule's own conclusion in prose, so a glossary would restate the input and add a variable to an experiment trying to hold everything but the producer still. No mention of which scenario is being looked at, ever. And no instruction to sound human or to match the mechanical producer's voice, which would be tuning for the grader rather than for the analyst.

A measurement this project relied on turned out to describe a measuring script rather than the system.

Sending evidence to an outside service is the one place this project lets data leave the machine, and the permission to do it rests on a measurement: exactly which fields go, listed one by one. No aircraft position reaches a package at all; an aircraft reference carries a pseudonym and a timestamp and nothing else. That list is what the clearance conversation was settled on.

Building this stage showed that the list had been produced by a dictionary written inside the measuring script, and that nothing in the system itself knew how to package an evidence bundle for sending. The measurement was about a document that existed only while the measurement was running. Unless the new code happened to reproduce it exactly, the thing measured and the thing sent would be two different documents that nobody would ever compare.

This is a different kind of fault from the three measuring-instrument faults already recorded here, and harder to catch. Those produced a wrong number. This produced a right number about the wrong object — and every check you could run on the measurement passes. The packaging now lives in the system, where the automatic checks see it, and the measurement re-runs against it.

Writing the test that holds the package to the evidence bundle's own field list then forced a decision that had never actually been made: two fields were missing from the measured list not because anyone chose to leave them out, but because the person writing the dictionary did not think of them. Both are internal bookkeeping — a database key and some row numbers — and leaving them out is right. What changed is that it is now a decision written down and argued for, and a third omission has to be argued for in the same place.

What stands between this and the first real request

None of it is code, and none of it should be skipped.

  • A dated model name, pinned. Services offer both a moving name that always points at the current version and a fixed one that never changes. Using the moving one means the study could be half-run against one model and half against another, with nothing in the results saying so. The system now refuses an undated name outright. Which specific model is a purchasing decision, not a design one.
  • A hard spending cap set at the service's own console. The limit inside the code is built and counts every request — but it lives in the software it is guarding, and it is held in memory, so a process that dies and restarts begins counting from zero. The cap at the other end is the one that works when the code is the broken thing. Somebody has to go and set it.
  • A key, in the environment and never in the repository. The slot exists and the system refuses to start without it rather than quietly running unauthenticated.
  • A hand-read trial on three packages before anything is run in bulk. It is also what confirms the assumption above about which rules the constraint mechanism will and will not accept — that is documentation rather than something this project can verify on its own.

Checking what it says against what it was shown

Everything above fixes the shape of an answer. It says nothing whatever about whether the things named inside it are real. A model can return a flawlessly formed explanation that rests on a receiver nobody mentioned, a rule that never ran, or a kind of finding nothing reported — correct in every respect except the one that matters. That is the failure this whole part of the project was built around, and shape-checking cannot touch it.

So every answer is now checked automatically before a person sees it. Each thing the explanation claims to be relying on has to be something the package actually contained.

Why that is even possible

Because of a decision made two stages earlier. The model is handed one sealed package and nothing else. Asking does this refer to something real? against twenty million stored readings has no answer anybody could compute. Asking it against the thirty-odd items in one package is a lookup. The sealed package was not built for tidiness; it is what makes the safeguard possible at any price, and a system that handed a model a database connection instead would have no equivalent available to it.

The trap, which an honest decision created

A package carries a second compartment beside its evidence: the explanations the system itself considered and threw out — a competing conclusion another rule bid for the same place and lost. They are in there deliberately, so a model can write a real alternative instead of pretending none existed.

what one package contains evidence RF-03, channel 4 — power rose 8.4 dB above its own baseline may be cited RF-03 — the broken-receiver verdict may be cited RF-02 — measured, and stayed normal may be cited rejected a data-source problem, ruled out in favour of the above NOT citable

Now consider a model that writes “this is a broken receiver, and it is supported by the data-source problem the system also found.” Every identifier in that sentence is in the package. A check that merely confirmed the names existed would wave it straight through — and would thereby certify that two rules agreed, when what actually happened is that two rules disagreed and one was thrown out.

The safeguard would have stamped its approval on precisely the mistake it exists to catch.

And nothing about the failure would look wrong from the outside: a well-formed explanation, a citation that resolves, a check that passes. This was spotted when the package's contents were agreed rather than after the fact, which is the only reason it was designed out rather than discovered later in a result nobody could explain.

So the check gives three answers rather than two. The claim is supported by the evidence; or it names nothing in the package at all; or it reaches for something the system already rejected. The third is treated as its own kind of failure and never folded into the second, because they are different faults: naming something that does not exist is invention, while citing a rejected conclusion is a misreading of the document the model was handed. The second is rarer and worse, and counting them together would bury it.

There is a symmetry that makes this coherent rather than merely strict. The rejected explanation is supposed to be mentioned — in the part of the answer that discusses alternatives, which is why it is sent to the model at all and why the instructions label it as rejected. It is only a failure when it appears in the part that lists supporting evidence. The same fact is welcome in the paragraph that is prose and forbidden in the field that can be checked.

What it deliberately cannot do

It checks that a citation points at something present. It cannot check that the sentence written beside that citation is a true description of it. A model can correctly name a real finding and then say something false about it, and this will pass it.

That boundary decides how the resulting number should be read, so it is worth stating plainly rather than leaving for someone to discover: the figure measures invented references and says nothing about misdescribed ones. Judging the sentence is what the human scoring is for, and the write-up must not slide between the two claims.

How do you check the checker?

By giving it an input whose correct answer is already known. The mechanical explainer described earlier copies every word it writes straight out of the package, so it cannot cite something absent. Running the check over its work is therefore a test with the answer written on the back: anything flagged there is a fault in the check, not in the explainer.

That only works under a condition which is easy to miss. Both pieces of software identify a finding the same way — by which rule produced it and what kind of finding it was. Had they shared the code that works that out, a mistake in it would have been invisible: the explainer would produce wrongly-labelled citations, the check would resolve wrongly-labelled citations, the two would agree perfectly, and the test would come back clean while both halves were wrong. A test that can share the fault it is testing for is not a test, so the two are now held apart automatically and the separation is verified rather than promised.

Asking a real model, and what three answers cost

Everything above was built without a model ever being asked anything. That was deliberate, and the point at which it stopped being deliberate arrived this week: the last gate was a hand-read trial on three packages, before anything runs in bulk.

The model, and why that one

The obvious criteria are price and recency, and both are wrong here.

Price is immaterial at this size. A twenty-package run costs somewhere between four pence and twenty-five, depending on the model. The gap between the cheapest sensible choice and the dearest is smaller than the cost of one mistake, so optimising it would be optimising the wrong thing.

Newest is a risk rather than a virtue. Two properties are needed and neither is universal: the ability to constrain the output's shape at generation time, and honouring a setting that tells the model not to be creative. Several newer models reject that setting outright. It matters more than it sounds, because the promise that somebody could re-run this work and get the same answers depends on it — and a model that silently ignores the setting does not fail, it varies while the record says it was pinned.

So the choice was made on those two properties, and the version is pinned to a dated one. Services offer a name that always points at the current version; this system refuses it. Half a study run against one model and half against its replacement, with nothing in the results saying so, is the failure that would be hardest to detect afterwards.

A trap sits exactly where anyone would look.

The published price list shows the moving names, not the fixed ones — so it appears to offer almost no pinnable versions at all. The few it does show are older models that cannot constrain output shape, which is the one thing this whole arrangement depends on. Choosing from the price list, the obvious place to start, yields precisely the models that cannot do the job. The real list lives somewhere else entirely.

The four questions the trial existed to answer

Three packages rather than twenty, and the answers printed in full to be read rather than checked automatically. That distinction is the point: an automated run can tell you an answer had the right shape and cited real evidence. It cannot tell you whether the words are any good. Somebody has to read three and decide.

It is eight answers, not eighty, and says nothing yet about how often it will happen at scale.

What reading them actually found

Two things, and neither would have surfaced from an automated check.

The blind marking was quietly broken, and by something nobody had specified.

The whole comparison depends on a second marker not knowing which explanation came from the model and which from the mechanical writer. The mechanical one lists one entry per kind of evidence — four, always. The model listed one per individual finding: ten on a small package, and it would have been thirty-four on a large one.

Four against thirty-four is not a hint, it is a certainty. A marker who noticed would know which was which every single time, and the result would have looked like unusually strong agreement between markers rather than like a broken instrument.

The cause was an instruction that had never been written. The output format defines a citation as standing for a kind of evidence, and the mechanical writer does that, and the instructions given to the model simply never said. So the measured difference between the two would have included a presentational habit that has nothing to do with the question being asked. One added rule fixed it: the same package now produces four citations instead of ten, and the model summarises across findings rather than repeating them — which reads better than the mechanical version does on identical evidence.

The second is subtler and cannot be fixed by an instruction. On one package the model cited a ratio as supporting evidence — four aircraft dropouts inside the window and none outside — while elsewhere in the same answer noting that this comparison could not be measured, because the period it was compared against contained nothing at all. Both statements are faithful to the package. The citation points at something real, so the automatic check passes it. Whether the thing it says about that evidence is reasonable is a judgement no machine here makes, and it is exactly the limitation recorded when the safeguard was built — now with a real example attached rather than a hypothetical one.

The answers do not reproduce exactly, and that changes a claim on this page

The trial was run more than once with identical inputs. The results were not identical.

Across five comparable pairs, four differed and one matched. Titles and summaries came back word for word; what varied was the free prose — the alternatives and the recommended action — and on one package the set of evidence the model chose to cite changed between runs. Once, an alternative explanation ended with a specific test that would disprove it; on the re-run, the same alternative simply stopped.

So "pin the version, turn off creativity, and anyone can reproduce these answers" is not true, and this page said it was.

What reproduces is the method: the same package, the same instructions, the same version, and answers of the same shape making the same claims. The wording does not, and occasionally the substance of a secondary field does not either. That is a weaker claim than the one intended and it is the true one.

It settles something else in the process. When a model's answer cannot be used, the system asks again before falling back — and the worry recorded when that was built was that asking again sends an identical request, so a machine that always answers identically would charge twice for the same refusal. It does not always answer identically. Asking again is a real mechanism, and that was the last unmeasured figure in this part of the system.

What it cost, and a small surprise

Eight answers across four runs: about eight pence. The system's own estimate came to about eleven, so the estimate is running high by roughly a fifth — which is the safe direction, and the likeliest explanation is that providers charge less for the parts of a request they have seen before, and every request here begins with the same instructions.

One number on this page was wrong in the other direction. A full twenty-package run was estimated at thirty-three thousand units of text; measured, it is closer to a hundred and thirty thousand. The original figure measured the evidence package and forgot that a request also carries the instructions and the shape definition, and that packages vary threefold in size. The conclusion is unchanged and now rests on better arithmetic: a whole run costs about thirty pence, so the spending guard exists for a runaway loop and never for the run itself.

The run that asks twenty questions

Every part needed to ask one question now exists and is measured. What did not exist was the job that asks twenty of them and comes out with an account honest enough to publish a number from. Building that job is where the interesting failures were — not in the asking, but in what gets kept.

The job is simple to describe. Take the twenty evidence packages chosen in advance. For each one, ask the model and ask the mechanical writer. Save every answer. Attach to each saved answer the verdict of the safeguard that checked it. Come out with a list of what was asked, what answered, what it cost, and where each answer was put.

Answers get thrown away, and the thrown-away ones are the evidence

When a model's answer cites something the package does not contain, the safeguard rejects it, the question is asked again, and if it fails a second time the mechanical writer stands in. The analyst sees one explanation. But the rejected answer is the only evidence the safeguard ever did anything — it is the row that says "a model claimed this, and it was not true".

The interface every producer answers through hands back one explanation. That is the right shape for a screen and the wrong shape for a campaign: asked through it, the rejected answers simply vanish. And nothing complains. Every package still gets an explanation, the catch rate still reports a number — a number with no records behind it. The fix is that the campaign asks through the longer route that returns the full trail, and the object holding the assembled producer now declares that route in its own description so the short one cannot quietly come back.

Three faults in the store that two careful readings had missed

The table an explanation is saved in was designed twice before anything wrote to it — once before the shape of an explanation was settled, and once six days ago when the two were compared field by field and four problems fixed. Both passes were careful. Both were done by reading. Building the first real writer found three more in an afternoon, and they have a shape in common: each is something the table could express and no code could produce.

The column with three states could only ever be given one of them.

Whether a claim was checked has three honest answers: it passed, it failed, nobody has looked yet. That third state was argued for at length and built deliberately, because a yes/no flag makes failed and not yet examined the same value. The writer set it to "not yet examined" and nothing ever came back to change it. A full run would have filled the table with explanations whose verdicts the run held in its hand and stored nowhere — which is worse than the flag it replaced, because the column now looks capable of the distinction while the data never makes it, and anyone checking the design rather than the contents would conclude it was being kept.

The third fault is the one no amount of reading could have found, because it only exists when both producers answer the same package — which is what a scored run is and what nothing before it did. When the model fails and the mechanical writer stands in, the saved row honestly records that the mechanical writer produced the text. The run also asks the mechanical writer for the baseline on that same package. Same writer, same input, no randomness: two rows identical to the character, one of which is what the model arm delivered.

Nothing in either row said which. So the rate at which the model had to be replaced was a number with no records behind it; the per-producer comparison was ambiguous at exactly the packages carrying the most weight; and the planned screen warning — a degraded explanation must look different from a real one, or the analyst cannot tell the layer has failed — was impossible to build rather than merely unbuilt.

The remedy is a second column recording which producer was asked, beside the one recording which answered. What it deliberately does not do is refuse rows where the two differ: differing is what a fallback is. Only one combination is nonsense — the mechanical writer named as the one asked while a model answered, since nothing falls back away from the thing both fallbacks fall back to — and that one is refused. All of it was free because the table still held no rows at all, which is the same window an earlier set of renames used, and that window is now shut.

A count that is easy to double, and a rate that would have looked fine

How often the safeguard catches something is a reported result of this project, not a maintenance statistic. Two ways of getting it wrong both produce a complete, plausible number.

  • Counting each answer twice. The safeguard is already run once per attempt by the machinery that decides whether to ask again. A run that then asked the same instrument for a verdict to save would add a second entry for an answer it had just counted — doubling the population and halving the published rate. The verdict that gets saved therefore comes from a check that records nothing, and the count is fed from exactly one place per producer.
  • Counting the stand-in. A mechanical explanation always passes, by construction. So counting stand-ins against the model would dilute the model's rate with the baseline's — in the direction that makes the model look better the more often it failed.

And the stand-in is not marked as checked at all, because nothing checked it: the fallback deliberately does not examine what it falls back to. "Nobody has looked yet" is the true answer there, and it is the first time that third state has carried real data.

What stops a run, and what it must survive

A package the model cannot answer is recorded and skipped, because a run that dies on the fourteenth package of twenty has spent the money and kept nothing. But two things stop it outright: hitting the spending guard, and sending a request the provider rejects. Both would otherwise be caught by the machinery designed to recover from a bad answer, and the result would be a run that quietly finished every remaining package on mechanical output while every saved row reported itself as a model's. Stopping still hands back the account of what was already saved — the same argument that makes a skipped package recorded rather than swallowed.

One more rule, and it is about honesty rather than mechanics. The run cannot choose its own packages. The twenty are fixed in advance and handed in; a run able to select them could select them after seeing how the first few turned out.

Asking the same question three times, and a limit that is not about money

The plan said each package goes to the model once. That rested on an assumption the first real requests disproved, and replacing it turned into a decision about a consent form.

The original argument was clean: the model is pinned to its least-random setting with a fixed seed, so asking twice returns the same words, and a second copy of an answer cannot inform a score. Running the trial twice showed four of five repeated pairs came back different — once with a different set of cited evidence, once with a caveat that had quietly gone missing. So one answer is a sample, not the answer. And the variation lands squarely on two of the five things the markers are asked to judge: what evidence an explanation leans on, and which alternatives it raises.

The obvious fix was rejected, and the reason was not the bill.

Marking all three answers is the statistically obvious move, and three passes over twenty packages costs about 80p of the four pounds left. But it takes the second marker from 40 explanations to 80, from 280 individual judgements to 560, and from about ninety minutes to over three hours. That ninety-minute figure is on the ethics consent form. Quietly tripling what a volunteer agreed to is a re-consent, not a setting — so the money said yes and the consent form said no, and the consent form wins.

What was chosen instead: ask three times, mark the first, and measure the other two. The marking corpus is untouched at twenty packages, forty explanations and 280 judgements, so nothing quoted to the participant moves. The two unmarked answers become a measured answer to "how much does this vary" — which was previously going to be either an assumption or a separate probe over a handful of packages.

Two rules had to be fixed at the same moment, because both are worthless if decided later. The marked answer is the first — not a random pick, and certainly not one chosen by any measurement, which is cherry-picking with a procedure attached. And it is not swapped when the model failed: if the first answer is one the mechanical writer had to stand in for, that stands as the marked answer, because it is what the model arm delivered. Swapping in the second would delete every failure from the marked set and flatter the system under test — a decision that looks perfectly reasonable if you make it after noticing a package fell back, which is exactly why it is written down first.

One consequence reaches the marker's own instructions. On a package where the model failed, both explanations under it are the mechanical one, word for word. Told nothing, a marker meeting that would assume the pack was broken. The handout now says two explanations under one package may be identical, that this is expected, and to mark each on its own terms — the least that keeps the instrument usable without hinting which is which.

Both changes are written into the instrument's own change log, with their reasons, before a single scored answer exists. An instrument that demonstrably predates its results is the whole point of writing it down in advance; one that is quietly wrong is worse than one that records its own amendments. The same commit fixed a correction that had been owed for six days: the plan described drawing five packages from each of four kinds of incident, which the built system cannot do — five kinds of incident produce only two kinds of package, and one of the four produces none at all.

Two failures that a fully passing test suite could not have predicted

The first attempt died after one paid request, complaining about a column that did not exist. The store the system actually runs against was two updates behind. The test suite builds its own copy of the store from scratch every time, brings it fully up to date, and passes — so fifteen hundred passing tests said precisely nothing about the one the real system opens. There is now a check that compares the two before the first request is paid for, because a check that runs afterwards is a note in a log, not a guard.

The second attempt lost five explanations, two of them from the marked set. The provider had been turning requests away for coming too fast, and the system recorded that as "the provider is unavailable" — which is wrong in a way that costs data. The refusal literally said "try again in five seconds". Treating something temporary as permanent quietly shrank a set of questions that had been fixed in advance, and nothing in the output said so.

The fix was to slow down rather than to retry — eleven seconds between requests — and that choice is not laziness. The system keeps two quite different reasons for switching away from the model firmly apart: there was no answer, and the answer could not be used. Retrying inside the run would have smeared one into the other, and every per-producer number this study reports depends on being able to tell them apart.

The first rebuild produced a properly formed document — and it was still a wall. One package went from about thirty-five lines to fifteen with every single value still on the page — and doing it revealed two things the flat list had hidden: the interference had hit the receivers almost exactly evenly, and two aircraft had dropped out twice each, invisible among fourteen lines that all read identically.

There is a trap in tidying up evidence, and the rule guarding against it had to be corrected. Had the pack gone one step further and summarised — "fourteen deviations between 8.4 and 15.0 decibels" — it would have done a piece of the thinking the marker is there to score. The reason first written down for forbidding that was that it would flatter the weaker explanation and narrow the measured gap. That was backwards. The mechanical writer's own account is a summary, so a pack that summarises first makes it read as pure repetition and scores it lower — while also taking credit away from the model for the same reason. Both move, by amounts nobody can estimate, which is a better reason to forbid it than the one given: a known bias can be argued about, an unknown one cannot.

The rule that replaced it is one a machine can check rather than one a person has to remember: the pack may rearrange numbers and may never work one out. Every measurement on the page has to appear word for word in something the detection rules produced. The one exception is counting the things the pack itself lists — allowed because the reader could count them, and needed because a marker checking a claim of "nine aircraft" has to be able to. No averages, no ranges, no totals. A test now reads every number on the page and fails if one of them came from nowhere.

A glossary, and the sentence it is not allowed to write

The packs are full of words nobody outside this project would know: rate excess, smoothing constant, nominal, disc, corroborate. A marker meeting those for the first time can stop and ask, or guess. Guessing is the worse outcome, because it is invisible — the returned sheet looks exactly the same either way, so a score nobody could interpret is indistinguishable from a careful one.

So the pack now opens with twenty-two definitions, repeated as a tab in the scoring spreadsheet — one place for while they are reading, one for while they are scoring.

A glossary is more dangerous than it sounds, and the danger is one sentence long.

Compare two ways of explaining the same term:

  • "One aircraft's position reports stopping for longer than the rule allows." — a definition.
  • "…which can indicate interference in the area." — the answer.

The second sentence is precisely what a good explanation was supposed to work out for itself, and precisely what the marker is being asked to judge — twice, once for whether the explanation reasons from its evidence and again at the end for whether it identified the right cause. Putting it in the glossary would move part of the test into the answer sheet, where nobody reading the results would think to look for it.

So the rule is: a definition says what a word refers to, never what it would mean if you saw it. And it needed a guard of its own, separate from the one about inventing numbers, for a reason worth noticing — a summary is arithmetic, and reasoning in prose is not. The number check would never have caught a sentence. There is now a list of giveaway phrases — "because", "indicates", "suggests", "due to", "likely" — that no definition is allowed to contain, checked against every entry.

One further rule turned up a real fault within seconds of being switched on. If a pack mentions a rule the glossary does not define, the pack refuses to build rather than quietly leaving a gap — because a gap looks like nothing at all until a marker hits it on page nineteen. The first time that ran, it stopped on a name the test suite had been using for months and the real system has never produced. Three sets of tests had been checking behaviour against a word that does not exist, and correcting it broke none of them, which is its own comment on how much those particular checks were doing. The thing that noticed was a glossary written for a human being.

Changing the marker's pack means changing a document that was deliberately fixed in advance, so it is written into that document's change log with its reasoning rather than quietly improved. It is defensible only because nobody has marked anything yet. The second marker is away until the middle of October, so not a single score exists to be invalidated. Had one sheet come back, the right answer would have been to leave the pack alone and record its unreadability as a limitation of the study.

One more near-miss is worth admitting, because it very nearly changed a result rather than a layout. The new score sheet first offered only the three scores the instrument describes in words — one, three and five. The instrument also says, in a sentence a few lines further down, to use two and four for anything in between. Since the agreement between the two markers is measured in a way that depends on how far apart their scores are, a form quietly refusing the in-between values would have changed the headline statistic while looking like nothing more than a tidier form. The test that was supposed to catch it had been written from the same wrong memory as the code.

How quickly, and how quietly

A correction that applies to every detection figure on this page. The three hundred hours all of them were first measured on were destroyed on 19 September by a test run pointed at the live store. A hundred were remade on 28 September for the model comparison and the remaining two hundred on 29 September, and every measurement was then re-run over the three hundred that now exist. Every conclusion held, and at the same corpus size the figures came back within a point or two of the ones that were lost — on seeds drawn independently of the originals, so this is the results reproducing rather than being recovered. Six of the ten headline figures are identical. The two that are not moved in opposite directions and both are in the working notes, not just the flattering one.

Worth admitting plainly: for eleven days the measurement logs in this repository described a corpus that was not in the database, and nothing anywhere said so. Every automated check passed throughout, because a saved log is a text file and nothing compares it to anything.

Two questions about the detection half had never been answered, and both turned out to be questions about definitions rather than about code. How fast does it notice something? And — the one everything else rests on — does it stay quiet when there is nothing there?

A delay in a system that never notices anything

The obvious definition of detection latency is the time between the incident starting and the system spotting it. Written down that way it cannot be measured here, because there is no moment of spotting: the rules read a completed hour out of the store afterwards. Nothing happens while the incident is happening. Had this been built before the definition was settled, the measurement would have reached for the nearest available timestamp — and the nearest one is when the evidence package was written to the database, which is the real clock on the machine wearing the costume of simulated time. It would have produced a table of plausible numbers that were actually measuring how busy the laptop was.

What is real, and what a live version of this system would face too, is how much of an incident has to elapse before the evidence a rule demands is complete. That is a property of how the rule is designed, not of how fast the software is, and it is a floor on what any faster implementation could achieve. The figure is reported as that, and never as a speed.

One detail decided the whole thing, and it came from reading the two rules rather than from thinking about time. The broken-receiver rule needs one receiver to have misbehaved for long enough. The interference rule needs more than one, and the window it reports runs from the first receiver's start to the last one's end. So at the moment that window opens, only one receiver has begun — and the rule's entire claim is that several went wrong together. A single formula for both would have understated interference delay by however long the receivers were staggered, silently and in the flattering direction.

Zero is the answer we want, and also the answer a broken machine gives

Everything the detection chapter claims depends on one unglamorous number: how often the system raises an alarm when nothing was staged. Without it, a catch rate means nothing — you can catch everything by alarming at everything. So there are sixty hours in the corpus with nothing staged in them, and the requirement is that none of them produce anything an analyst would be shown.

Checking that looks like it needs no code at all: look at the list of alarms, confirm it is empty. That is the trap. An empty list is what a working system produces on a quiet hour. It is also what you get from a system that never ran, that was pointed at the wrong hours, or that had everything thrown away by a filter before the detectors ever saw it. All four look identical from the outside, and three of them are failures.

Only the teal path is the system working. Nothing about the result distinguishes it from the three dashed ones, because the thing being checked is an absence — so the check had to be given something other than the result to look at.

The fix is to change what the check is handed rather than what it looks for. It receives an account of every detector by name — one entry each, saying what that detector produced — and it refuses to grade the hour at all if any detector is missing from the account. A detector that found nothing has to say so. It cannot simply fail to turn up. That refusal, and not any count, is what answers did this detector actually look.

The result: none of the sixty quiet hours produces anything an analyst would be shown. That is the number every catch rate in the project is quoted against.

The two halves of the data disagree about what silence means

Underneath the three rules an analyst sees are simpler ones that do the looking. Here the expectation reverses: they are supposed to find things on a quiet hour, and finding nothing is the symptom of a detector that has stopped working. The aircraft tracks are real recorded traffic, and real traffic is full of ordinary dropouts — aircraft leaving receiver range, transponders going quiet, ground stations missing reports. A quiet hour genuinely contains between eight and twenty-two of them.

That rule was nearly written into the check as a blanket one, and it would have been wrong for half the system. The radio grid is invented rather than recorded. On a quiet hour nothing is injected into it, so there is genuinely nothing there to deviate — and, measured, the radio rule finds nothing on sixty hours out of sixty. A blanket "you must find something" rule would have failed every correct hour in the corpus, and failed them in a way that looks like a detection fault rather than a rule written too broadly.

A test that was never built cannot be credited with staying quiet

There are three answers the system is allowed to give, and only two have tests behind them. The third — we are not hearing aircraft here — was attempted twice, measured twice, and closed both times: the signal is not separable from ordinary background on hours generated this way, and the version that would work needs the whole corpus regenerated and every detection figure re-measured.

So a report saying zero of these across sixty quiet hours would be quoting the absence of a test as the accuracy of a test. How well it did, and what every number means

Everything up to here says what the system decided. This says how well — and it is the last piece of evidence the detection half needed. Until now three of its six figures had never been computed at all.

The definitions are the hard part, not the arithmetic. Five of the six figures are fractions, and a fraction means nothing without its denominator. Two of the denominators here are so easily confused that the same English word carries both — and on one kind of hour they come out forty-one points apart, with both answers correct. So every figure below is written as a count over a named population, and never as a bare percentage.

The six figures, defined

Recall
Of the hours that were a given kind, the fraction on which the system gave the right answer. Counted by what was staged. It is the answer to did we miss anything? and it says nothing whatever about how often the system was wrong when it did speak — you can score a perfect recall by shouting on every hour.
Precision
Of the hours the system called a given thing, the fraction that really were it. Counted by what was claimed. It is the answer to can we believe it when it speaks? and says nothing about what it stayed silent through. Its population is a different set of hours from recall's — that is the whole reason the two are in separate tables below rather than in one.
F1
The two above combined into one number, so that neither can be improved by sacrificing the other. Because it joins a kind of hour to a kind of answer, it only exists where one corresponds to one. Where two kinds of hour produce the same answer, an F1 would be a score with no subject, and it is withheld rather than fudged.
False alarms
Of the hours where the right answer was silence, the fraction on which the system said something anyway. There are two of these and they are never added together — an alarm on an empty hour and an alarm provoked by something real but unrelated are different failures, and one combined figure cannot say which occurred.
Misses
The mirror of recall: of the hours that were a given kind, the fraction the system failed to name. Reported because it is what an operator actually cares about, and kept explicitly rather than left for the reader to subtract.
Delay
How much of an incident had to elapse before the evidence a rule demands was complete. It exists only for the hours the system got right, so it describes the successes and not the kind of hour — printed beside a recall of less than one it invites exactly the wrong reading. It is reported with the number of hours it was averaged over.

All three ranges are identical — ten seconds to ninety — and only the median separates them. The shaded strip on the left is forbidden ground: no rule in the system will act on evidence shorter than ten seconds, so no delay can land there. Both broken-receiver medians sit exactly on that edge.

The ten-second floor is not a rounding artefact and not a coincidence. A blip lasting a single reading stopped counting as an event earlier in this project, which means ten seconds is the shortest stretch of evidence any rule will act on. No delay can be shorter than that, and the two broken-receiver rows sit exactly on it: the rule decided as soon as it was permitted to look. That threshold was chosen to remove false reports, and this is the first time the project has put a price on it — the price is that nothing can be known faster.

The interference row is one sampling interval slower, and that is the rule's claim showing up in the arithmetic rather than a weakness. Interference means more than one receiver went wrong together, so the earliest moment it can be supported is when the second receiver's evidence becomes sufficient — not the first. A single formula for both rules would have understated this by however long the receivers were staggered.

Two possibilities the definition allowed for turned out not to occur: no delay came out negative — which would have meant a rule sweeping in readings from before the incident began — and there was no hour where a rule announced something but its evidence could not be traced.

A perfect score is a weaker result than it looks

Every figure in the tables above is 1.000 or 0.000, and that is not as good as it sounds. What it establishes is that the detection layer is correctly wired and internally consistent. It does not establish that the layer would detect real interference.

Three reasons, and they belong in the write-up rather than in a footnote.

  • The radio half of the data is invented. The receiver grid, the way signals fall off with distance, and the incident itself are all generated by this project, and the injected disturbance sits comfortably above the detection threshold by construction. A precision of 1.000 measures the rules against the generator that fed them.
  • There are no near-misses in the corpus. Nothing in it stages an incident at the edge of what can be detected, so no hour in it is genuinely difficult. A result about telling hard cases apart needs a body of hours that contains hard cases, and building one is the obvious next experiment rather than a rerun of this one.
  • One of the three answers has no rule behind it. The figures cover two, which is why the coverage-gap hours are a silence to be checked rather than a fourth thing to be caught, and why every rate above is quoted as covering two of three.

There is one figure in the whole results section that carries information from outside the generator, and it is worth saying which: the per-aircraft recall. The aircraft tracks are real recorded traffic rather than invented, so the disruptions staged on them have to compete with genuine loss of contact, aircraft on the ground, and ordinary gaps in coverage. That figure is 0.587 on two of the three kinds of hour it applies to — and the accounting for where the missing aircraft went is complete, with two of the four suspected causes responsible for none of them at all.

The account closes exactly: 599 flagged and 301 missed, every one of the 301 attributed. Three other causes that had been suspected — the aircraft leaving the area covered, disappearing from the record entirely, or having a gap that could not be bracketed at all — turned out to account for none of them. The two rows differ almost entirely in the first two categories, and the lower row is one measurement shown twice: those two kinds of hour disrupt aircraft the same way, from the same starting number, so they are not two results.

The honest summary, and the one that will be written up: the detection layer does exactly what it was specified to do on the hours it was given, it never confuses one kind of incident with another, it never speaks when it should be silent, and the question of how it behaves against genuine interference at the edge of detectability remains open.

Every number above can be followed back to what produced it

A results table is only as good as its provenance, and until now every figure in this project lived in a saved log — text a person reads and retypes. Once a number has been retyped into a table it has lost every connection to the run that produced it. So the figures above also exist as data files, and the point of them is not the format.

The unit that actually travels into a dissertation is one row, pasted into a table, arriving without any line that was above it. A header block naming the corpus and the settings would be lost at exactly that moment. So every row carries a short fingerprint of the run description as its first column, and one file resolves it:

Change any part of that description and the fingerprint changes with it, so a row exported under older settings fails to resolve rather than quietly pointing at the wrong run.

A seed reproduces the hours; a setting reproduces the figures

This was the correction that building the export forced, and it applies to how the results chapter must be written. The project had been treating "record the number the hours were generated from" as what makes a figure reproducible. It is not.

The generating number fixes which three hundred hours exist. It says nothing at all about the settings the detectors were run at — how far from normal a reading has to be before it counts, how long it has to stay there, how long a gap in an aircraft's reports has to be, the three distances and the one ratio the final rules turn on. Set the first of those to eight decibels instead of six and every figure in every table above moves, on exactly the same hours, with the generating number unchanged.

Thirteen settings therefore travel with the results, read off the objects the measurement actually used rather than typed out beside it — a recorded setting that is not the one in force is worse than none, because it looks like provenance. A description missing any of them cannot be constructed, which is the difference between a rule that is checked and one that cannot be broken.

Why the blank cells are blank, and why that took care

Several cells in the exported tables are empty, and an empty cell is the most dangerous thing a results file can contain: a spreadsheet reads it as zero, and so does every data-analysis library. The tables above are careful that a rate over nothing is not measured rather than 0.000 — a precision of zero on an answer nobody ever gave would read as an answer that was always wrong — and that care evaporates the moment the number is written to a file.

Labelling the cell with a word was rejected: it makes the column non-numeric, and the whole purpose is files a chart can be drawn from. Instead no rate is ever written without the counts it was computed from in the columns beside it, so a blank can always be read:

Without the two count columns, all three of those rows would look identical to a reader, and two of them would look like a detector that found nothing.

One last rule, which sounds pedantic and is not: the export is forbidden to calculate anything. It rearranges values and derives none — no rounding, no percentages, no column totals. Rounded to three places on the way out, a file would say 0.823 where the measurement says 0.8233333333333334, and the number that gets quoted would no longer be the number that was checked. Rounding belongs in the document, where a reader can see it happening.

The rules the build follows

  • Nothing is written before it is specified and proven to fail. Every capability gets a numbered entry with its inputs, outputs and at least one way it must fail; then tests; then the tests are run and watched to fail; only then the code.
  • Confidence is Low, Medium or High. Never a percentage, never a score. It applies everywhere a number could creep in.
  • Nothing is silently dropped. Every record leaves the front door as either an observation or a documented rejection, and both counts are reported.
  • Every record knows where it came from. Provenance is attached centrally, so a new source cannot forget it.
  • Detection is deterministic. Written rules, not learned models — so a detection can always be explained by pointing at the rule that fired.
  • The AI never sees raw data. It receives a structured evidence bundle and nothing else, and never learns which scenario it is looking at.
  • Every claim it makes is checked against the evidence. Ungrounded claims are caught automatically, and how often that happens is itself a reported result rather than a hidden safety net.
  • The analyst is the last word. The system proposes; a person confirms, rejects, or asks for more.

Nothing it computed could be seen in it

Everything above this point is real. The rules work, the figures are measured, the packages are checked, the explanations are written. And until this week a person sitting in front of the system could see none of it. The question that found this was a short one — does the detector output on injected anomalies show in the app at all, or only in local measurement? — and the answer was no, for two separate reasons that each looked like somebody else's job.

Two gaps, and neither could fail a test

The first is that nothing in the running system ever called the detectors. The order the eight rules run in — what normal is, then the radio rule, then the rule that judges whether a receiver is broken, then the aircraft rule, then the two map questions, then the interference rule, then the decision that picks between them — existed in exactly one place: a measurement script. Measurement scripts sit deliberately outside the formatter, the type checker, the test suite and the coverage gate, so the arrangement behind every detection figure on this page was the one part of the chain that nothing checked, while all eight rules individually sat at full coverage.

The second is that the main screen had three panels with nothing behind them. A detection summary, a strip of radio receivers, and a timeline of events. Each was served an empty answer by the part of the system that feeds the screen, under a note written months earlier explaining why: no detector existed, no radio source existed, no generator existed, so an empty panel is honest and a full one would be invented data put in front of an analyst.

That note was correct when it was written and had quietly stopped being true. By the time it was read again there were eight detection rules, the invented radio source, the generator, a hundred and three stored evidence packages and a hundred and fifty-five stored explanations. Nothing noticed, and nothing could have. Three planned panels read those three fields and no piece of work anywhere was responsible for writing them, so every one of those panels could have been built, tested and passed while the fields stayed empty for a reason that had expired. A field that everything reads and nothing writes is invisible in a list of work.

Zero is the answer we want and also the answer a broken machine gives

Joining the chain up turned out to be the easy half. The hard half is a rule that reads like pedantry and is the whole of it: an hour nobody has examined must not look like an hour that was examined and found clean.

Findings are stored per hour per rule, and rewritten each time a rule runs — including when it finds nothing, which is deliberate, because findings can always be recomputed from the hour's starting number and leaving an old set beside a new one would make every count include both. The consequence is that four quite different situations leave an identical, empty table.

What happenedWhat is storedIs it a problem?
Examined, nothing wrongnothingNo — this is the correct answer for a quiet hour

Examined with the detectors pointed at the wrong hournothingYes

Three of the four are failures, and no number tells them apart, because every number is zero. This is the same argument the quiet-hour check makes one layer down, arriving at the screen.

So no count can answer the question did a detector look. What can is an account: a list of every rule that exists and what each one found, including the ones that found nothing. The presence of the list is the answer; the numbers in it are the result. An hour nobody examined has no list at all, and the screen says so in a sentence rather than showing a dash — a reader cannot tell a dash meaning nothing to report from a dash meaning nobody looked, and the second is a broken machine.

The list needs somewhere to live that survives an hour with no findings, so the hour itself is now stamped with when it was examined and the settings it was examined under. It is stamped last, after every other thing the pass writes: stamped first, a pass that died half way would leave an hour claiming a complete examination over a partial set of findings, and nothing on either side could detect that. Stamped last, the same crash leaves an hour that is not known to have been examined — which is true — and running it again is the whole of the repair.

One of the planned panels wants a count per kind of finding, and three of the counts cannot be numbers. They are missing for two quite different reasons, and collapsing them would misreport the system in opposite directions.

The third row covers two rules whose output is genuinely transient: one groups nearby aircraft dropouts and its reference to a place is too loose to be looked up again, and the other compares a rate inside a window against the same place outside it and produces a ratio rather than a located finding. Both looked. Neither leaves a row.

There is a related trap the panel avoids. A perfectly quiet hour genuinely holds about 138 aircraft reporting gaps, because aircraft stop broadcasting for ordinary reasons all the time — out of range, on the ground, transponder off. Those are working material and not conclusions, and an analyst shown "138" as an anomaly count would read a correct non-detection as a storm. Every rule's count travels with a label saying which kind it is, so the screen can show the conclusions and keep the rest as supporting detail.

The heading was announcing the answer

There is a standing rule in this project that the injected answer key never leaves the evaluation machinery. A detector that can see what was staged is not being tested, and an analyst who can see it is not being observed. The rule has a written failure case, and it names, almost word for word, the thing that feeds the main screen carrying the kind of hour the active run is.

That is what it had been doing. The largest text at the top of the analyst's screen was the run's scenario type, so for weeks it read gnss-degradation — the name of the thing deliberately injected into the data whose detectors are being measured against it.

The reason it survived is worth more than the fix. The record of a run was built with real care against exactly this: the object the screen's data comes from has no field for the answer key at all, on the recorded argument that a shape which cannot hold the answer cannot leak it on any run — stronger than remembering to strip it in each place. A second object carrying the incident's radius, height, offset and duration was deliberately kept separate, on the stated grounds that those would describe the staged incident to the very analyst whose detectors are being measured against it.

And the name of the kind of hour sat on the shared object the whole time. The distinction being run together is that the type is not the event; it is the name of the event — and a name that gives away the mechanism, the band being attacked, and the fact that anything was injected at all is not a weaker disclosure than the measurements that were so carefully withheld. "No field for the answer key" had been read as "no answer key".

Two things about this are worth carrying into the write-up. It is invisible to searching the whole project for the offending text, because the same words are entirely legitimate on the scenario screen — which is the harness's own face and exists to show what was generated — and disqualifying on the analyst's screen, which shows a detector's output. And every screenshot taken of that screen so far shows the answer, which makes it a threat to the validity of any future figure that depends on a person reading that screen. No such figure has been taken yet, which is the only reason this is a near miss rather than a retraction.

The replacement is deliberately dull: the heading now names the run by its number. And the check that replaced the old one does not look at that one field — it serialises the entire answer the screen receives and searches it for all five kinds of hour, because the written failure case says "as a field, a label, or anything from which it can be inferred", and a check on one field only ever proves the field somebody thought of.

How it was proved not to have changed anything

Moving the arrangement of eight rules out of a measurement script and into the system is exactly the kind of change that quietly alters a result. The check was to re-run the figures of record through the new arrangement over the same three hundred hours and compare the output byte for byte.

Of forty-three lines, one differed. It is the short code that stamps the record of which settings produced the figures, and it moved because the record gained something it had been missing: the list of quiet hours that "normal" was measured over. Every figure was identical. A stamp that had stayed the same while the record changed underneath it would have been the actual failure.

And now the pass starts itself

Everything above this point left one gap: a working pass, a screen that could show its output, and nothing that ever called the pass. So every hour in the store honestly reported itself as unexamined, and finishing a batch of hours changed nothing on screen. The piece that closes it is small and the arithmetic is the whole of its design.

Only the hours that batch made are examined — never everything in the store, which is six minutes and would rewrite the findings behind every figure on this page under whatever settings happen to be current. An examination that fails on one hour is recorded and stepped over, so the other nineteen are still examined; a failure that reached the batch would report a batch that had successfully made and stored twenty hours as a batch that failed.

And there is one situation where it refuses to examine anything at all. Working out what normal looks like is done over the quiet hours — the ones with nothing staged — and never over the hour being judged. Generate twenty interference hours into an empty store and there are no quiet hours to measure against, which is what a brand-new database always looks like. The alternative is measuring "normal" from the staged hours themselves, which would describe the incident as normal and report every hour clean. So it refuses, the hours stay made and stored, and each one reports itself unexamined — which the screen already states in a sentence.

Two commands that behave differently, on purpose

The button on the screen now scores what it generates. The command that built the three hundred hours behind every figure on this page deliberately does not.

Two things that do almost the same job and behave differently is normally a sign of a mistake. Here it is the point. If making an hour also examined it, the hour and the detector settings would be welded together — and the ability to take the same three hundred hours and re-read them under a different threshold, to see which figures move and which hold, is one of the few things this project can offer that a live system cannot. One command exists to produce evidence and must not fix the settings; the other exists to put something on a screen.

The difference is a matter of where the code sits rather than a setting somebody has to remember, and it is checked in both directions. "The hour-making command never reaches a detector" is a sentence that would also be true of a system with no detection in it at all, so the opposite is checked beside it.

Eleven numbers that lived in the wrong place

Building that trigger turned up something larger than the trigger. Every detection figure on this page means something only in combination with the settings it was measured under: how far a radio reading must sit from normal before it counts, and the two steps above that which separate a mild deviation from a severe one; how stale an aircraft's position may get, and how recently it must have been heard from; and the four edges of the box the aircraft rules apply over. Change the first of those from six decibels to eight and every catch rate, every precision figure and every delay above moves — on exactly the same hours, from exactly the same starting numbers.

Those eleven numbers existed in two places, both outside the system: the measurement script that produced the figures, and a copy in the tests annotated "exactly the settings of record". The system itself held none of them.

That is harmless for precisely as long as nothing inside the system runs a detector — and this is the thing that runs one. Left alone, the trigger would have declared a third set with the same standing as the other two, and the failure that follows is the quiet kind this project keeps meeting: three copies that have drifted apart are each perfectly consistent with themselves, so nothing anywhere breaks, and the screen simply shows figures taken under settings that differ from the ones every number above was measured under. There would be no symptom.

So the eleven moved into the system, and the script and the tests now read them from there. Worth saying what was not homeless, because it is most of why nobody had noticed: four of the six groups of settings were already sitting in the system at their measured defaults. A reader checking whether the system knew its own detector settings would have found them and reasonably concluded that it did.

One more mechanism, because moving them is not enough. Moving the numbers removes the copies and leaves a change to them completely silent — somebody edits a threshold, every test still passes, and every figure on this page is now quoted under settings that are no longer in force. So the eleven are also written out, separately, in a test. That looks like the duplication just removed and is not: one of them is what the detectors actually run on, and the other is a statement that those values are the ones the figures were measured under. Change one of the eleven and the statement fails and says so.

What still does not happen

The three hundred hours already in the store are still unexamined, and will stay that way until something examines them. They have findings and no record of being examined, which is the correct reading of them: they were examined by a measurement script, before anything recorded that examinations happen. The trigger covers new batches only. Re-examining the existing three hundred is about six minutes and is a command somebody runs once, deliberately rather than automatically — it is the thing that will finally put detection output on screen for the hours every figure here was measured on.

The explanations are still not served. A hundred and fifty-five of them are stored, and the panel that would show them is still deliberately empty — the last field on that screen that everything reads and nothing writes. It is named here rather than left silent, because that is precisely the lesson of the expired note at the top of this section.

A passing test is not automatically evidence

Several tests here assert that something is absent — that no database address appears anywhere in the source, that nothing builds a second connection pool, that a build command cannot silently succeed while doing nothing. A test like that passes on the day it is written and would keep passing if it asserted nothing at all.

So each of them was checked by deliberately breaking the thing it guards, confirming it goes red, and putting it back. One of those checks caught a real hole: a test looking for a forbidden address by searching the source text was satisfied by an address written in a comment, which is exactly how the same test had failed once before. It was rewritten to inspect the parsed code rather than the raw characters.

This week produced a new kind of it: a test that could only ever pass.

Two tests compared the readings that had been stored against the readings that had been produced — one checking they match for a quiet run, the other checking they differ for a staged one. Both comparisons were malformed in the same way: the stored side had been reduced to a timestamp and a reading, while the produced side still carried the labels a record picks up on the way in. The two were never the same kind of thing, so the first test could not pass however correct the code was, and the second could not fail however wrong it was.

Only one of them said anything. The equality test failed the moment there was code to run it against, and was investigated within a minute. Its twin had been green since it was written and would have stayed green through every deliberate breakage, including the one that mattered — a generator that stages nothing at all. The useless test was invisible precisely because the one beside it failed loudly.

This is a different fault from the text-matching ones elsewhere on this page. Nothing was being searched for as text; two descriptions of the same data were compared as though they were the data, and the difference between the descriptions was read as a difference in the readings. A test that can only ever pass is as worthless as one that can only ever fail, and far harder to notice — because everything is green.

"The tests failed" turned out to be three different facts. The first run of a new test file produced a single collection error, because the module under test did not exist yet. That satisfies "the tests fail" while establishing nothing — every test in the file fails that way regardless of what it asserts.

Running them against a stub that raises instead gave per-test results, and three of them were wrong: one caught the stub itself rather than the condition it claimed to test, one was satisfied by an empty function body, and two were the absence assertions above. All three were real defects in the tests, and none would have been visible from the red run alone.

Three ways for a test to be blind, and three different instruments

All of the above is about the back end, where a rule insists that the tests exercise at least nine parts in ten of the code and reports what they miss. The screens measure nothing of the kind. There is no such tooling on that side of the project at all, and last week it cost something concrete: three behaviours of the scenario screen turned out to have no test whatsoever, and nothing reported it.

So the navigation work was checked a different way — by breaking it on purpose, eight times over, one break at a time, and watching which tests went red. A fourth entry added to the rail. The address ignored, so a reload lands you somewhere else. Every entry claiming to be the current one. The listed runs presented with nothing saying they are a copy. All eight were caught, and each went red on the test written for it rather than on some distant assertion, which is the property worth having: a suite where one broken thing turns twenty tests red localises nothing.

The useful result was not the eight. It was counting which tests they had touched.

The first seven breakages were derived from the decisions written down before the work started, and between them they never disturbed one of the ten tests — the one asserting that choosing the scenario entry actually gets you to that screen. Breaking a decision cannot disturb the wiring the decision takes for granted. The eighth was written for that one specifically, once the tally showed the hole.

This is a real limit of the method, worth stating rather than glossing: breaking a system along the lines of what somebody wrote down tests the claims that were made and is blind to the assumptions nobody thought to state. Counting tests against breakages is what finds that; the breakages alone do not.

And then a third kind, found by neither. Opening the finished screen in a browser showed the little heading that labels the table of runs sitting underneath the column headings instead of above them — one word of styling, borrowed from a text field, that quietly takes a table caption out of the box the browser reserves for it.

Nothing could have caught it. The tests find that table by the name the caption gives it, and that name was correct however the caption was displayed — so they were passing for exactly the right reason, on exactly the element that was wrong. The tests run in a simulated browser that does no layout at all, and this project's own convention forbids writing tests that assert on styling rather than on what an analyst can see, which closes off the only check that would have found it. Three blind spots, three instruments: coverage finds behaviour nobody asserted, breaking things finds assertions that do not hold anything up, and looking at the running screen finds what is asserted correctly and drawn wrongly.

What it deliberately does not do yet

Each of these is scheduled work, not an oversight. They are listed so nobody oversells what exists.

  • Replay is a command, not a schedule. Someone still has to type it. A run now drives both of its halves itself, so the replay is no longer a separate manual step inside a scenario — but nothing runs on a timer, and nothing decides on its own that a batch is due.
  • Every run in a batch is configured identically. One command now produces twenty runs from one number, and they differ in where the incident lands, which aircraft are caught and which minutes of traffic are replayed — but not in how loud the interference is, how wide the zone reaches, or how long it lasts. Those are set once for the whole batch and changed between batches. Varying them run by run would change how hard each run is to detect, which interacts directly with the spread described above, so it is a decision to take deliberately rather than by default.
  • No explanation has ever been scored. The detection half is now marked against the answer key — every kind of hour decided correctly sixty times out of sixty. What has never been scored is the half the project is actually about: the quality of a written explanation, which needs a hypothesis layer to produce one and a second marker to judge it. The instrument for that is written and locked; nothing has been put through it.
  • A model has been asked, and nobody has judged the answers. Both writers have now produced an explanation for each of the twenty chosen packages, and the mechanical one can only choose between the explanations its rules were built to tell apart. Whether the model does better on identical evidence is the question the whole project exists to ask, and answering it means two people scoring forty explanations without knowing which writer produced which. Everything in front of that is done; the scoring is not.
  • The safeguard is built, and what it can check is narrow. Every claim an explanation rests on is now looked up in the package it came from, which is the check that constraining a model's output cannot be: the constraint guarantees the shape and says nothing about whether the things cited exist. What it does not check is the sentence attached to a real reference, so a true citation with a false description beside it passes. That makes its catch rate a floor on invention rather than a measure of it, and leaves misdescription entirely to the human marking.
  • One of the three screens does not exist, and the picture is still thinner than it looks — though no longer for the old reason. It used to be sparse because nothing produced what it was designed to display; it is now sparse because the two panels that would lay that out properly are not built, so what arrives is rendered plainly rather than well, and the timeline is carried in the answer and not drawn at all. The scenario screen is wired end to end, with two exceptions it states plainly: its reset and replay controls are disabled, and several of its settings have no effect for some scenario types without saying so.
  • The three hundred stored hours are still unexamined. The pass now starts itself when a batch of hours finishes, but that covers new batches only. The hours every figure on this page was measured on have findings and no record of being examined, which is the correct reading of them — they were examined by a measurement script, before anything recorded that examinations happen. Re-examining them is about six minutes and is a command somebody runs once rather than behaviour, which is a deliberate line: a stage that decided on its own to re-examine three hundred hours would rewrite the findings behind every figure here under whatever settings happened to be current.
  • Nothing decides on its own that anything is due. The pass starts when a batch finishes and at no other time. There is no timer anywhere in this project, no retry, and nothing that notices an hour has gone unexamined and does something about it. That is the same line as the first item on this list and it is held on purpose: everything that happens here happens because somebody asked for it.
  • Explanations are stored and not served. A hundred and fifty-five of them exist and the panel that would show them is deliberately empty. It is the last field on that screen that everything reads and nothing writes, and serving one raises questions that have not been settled: whose explanation an analyst sees when two writers produced one for the same package, and whether an explanation that failed its own citation check may be shown at all.
  • A batch that fails after it is accepted is invisible. Starting one answers immediately, so nothing carries a later failure back to the person who asked. It looks identical to a batch still running.
  • Replayed runs still carry a placeholder label. A generated run now writes a proper record of its own. A replay has no incident and no starting number to record, so it still opens a run labelled plainly as a replay — which has turned from a stopgap into a useful thing, since it is what tells replayed rows from generated ones at a glance.
  • Twenty packages is a small measurement, deliberately. The scored run covers twenty packages per writer, which is what two people can read carefully rather than what would settle a rate. It is enough to say whether the model invents references on this corpus — across eighty explanations the safeguard caught nothing — and nowhere near enough to put a confidence interval on anything, or to say whether either result holds across all five kinds of incident.
  • Nothing has been marked yet, and the marking is the result. The comparison this project exists to make — is a model's explanation better than an honest mechanical one over identical evidence — needs two independent people scoring both, without knowing which is which. The instrument is written and frozen; nobody has used it.
  • The comparison has been run and not yet scored. The twenty packages were drawn and written down before anything was asked, the run was made, and it cost eighty-five pence. What exists is eighty explanations and a reading pack; what does not exist is a single mark on any of them.
  • The hard cap at the service provider has not been set. The guard inside the code is built — it counts every request, records what each one cost, and stops the run rather than quietly continuing. The second guard is a cap set in the provider's own console, and it is the one that works when the code is the broken thing. It also covers something the in-code guard cannot: that limit is held in memory, so a process that dies and restarts begins counting from zero. It cannot be written; somebody has to go and set it, and no request should be made before that.
  • Nothing enforces quality on push. The checks all exist as commands; until they run automatically, a green result is evidence about one machine at one moment.

One threat to validity, and the mitigation that turned out not to exist

Inside the box there are ten genuine reporting gaps that happen while an aircraft is flying and well away from the edges. Seven of them last between 100 and 300 seconds, which is squarely the band the coverage-gap incident is going to stage. A real gap and a staged one in the same track would be indistinguishable, and the false-alarm rate for that kind of incident would mean nothing.

The mitigation was always obvious — the generator must refuse those tracks as places to stage an incident — and an earlier version of this page described it as bookkeeping, on the grounds that the tracks were already identified. They were not. The coverage report that produced the ten measures how long each gap lasted and discards which aircraft it belonged to, so the mitigation had no input at all. That would have been discovered at the moment the generator reached for the list, which is the expensive moment rather than the cheap one.

Producing the list properly also corrected the figure this page has been quoting. Ten gaps are not ten aircraft. Three of them carry two gaps each, so the confound touches 7 of 948 tracks — 0.74% of the fleet, not the 1% stated before. The list is now built alongside the archive itself, by the same command, and it holds pseudonyms rather than real aircraft addresses so it can be kept in the repository at all.

This threat is now dormant rather than retired, and the distinction is worth keeping. It was a threat to the false-alarm rate for coverage-gap incidents, and there is no such rate any more, because the test that would have produced one was closed. It comes straight back the moment the hours are made a third time to give coverage gaps a place of their own — which is the one change that would make that test work. The mitigation is built and the list exists; it is simply not currently doing anything. Recorded so that a later attempt inherits the finished work rather than rediscovering the problem.

What happens next

Next
Re-examining the three hundred hours already in the store. About six minutes, and it is what finally puts detection output on screen for the hours every figure on this page was measured on. A new batch made from the screen now examines itself; the existing hours predate anything that recorded examinations happening, so they read as unexamined, which is correct and is also why the screen still shows nothing found for them. It is a command run once on purpose rather than something the system decides, because a stage that re-examined three hundred hours on its own initiative would rewrite the findings behind every figure here under whatever settings happened to be current.

Alongside it, re-running the figures of record. The script that produces them now reads its settings from the system rather than holding its own copy, and builds what normal looks like through the same code the system uses. The values are identical and that equivalence is checked directly, but the previous change of this kind was proved by reproducing all forty-three lines byte for byte, and this one has not had that treatment yet.

Then the two panels that lay the detection answer out properly. Both now have something behind them for the first time; what the screen does with the answer today is deliberately plain.
Next
The marking, and it waits on a person rather than on code. Everything in front of it is done: the twenty packages drawn and written down, the run made, the pack rebuilt so it can actually be read.

What is left is two people scoring forty explanations blind, and then the arithmetic over what they return — joining their scores to the record of which writer produced what, only after both sheets are in, and reporting how much the two of them agreed. The second marker is away until the middle of October, so that is the date this waits on.

The arithmetic is deliberately not being built ahead of time, and the reason is not scheduling. What a returned sheet actually looks like is a fact about what two people did with a file — a blank cell, a pasted value, rows re-sorted, one sheet saved in another format — and none of that is knowable in advance. Built against an invented sheet, it encodes a tidy return that nobody promised. There is a second and stronger reason: the rule for settling disagreements between the two markers was fixed in advance, and writing the code that applies it while looking at the first returned sheet means making choices with partial results already visible. Waiting until both are in keeps that line where it can be seen.

One thing is worth stating now the numbers exist. The safeguard's catch rate is a result this project reports, not a maintenance statistic — and across eighty explanations it caught nothing at all. That is a genuine answer rather than an anticlimax: a model constrained in shape and handed a sealed package does not appear to invent references. It is also a narrow one, and the narrowness is the point — the safeguard checks that a named rule exists, never that the sentence attached to it is true. Which of those two the study can actually claim is what the marking decides.

One piece of code did proceed while that waits, and it is the table of detection figures above. The set of hours was deliberately finished first, so it was computed once over a fixed three hundred rather than computed, extended and recomputed with results already in view. Three of its six figures had never been computed at all before this, and the delay column had never been run against a single real hour.

What remains on that side is not another figure but the same figures over harder hours. Everything in the table is a 1.000 or a 0.000, which says the layer is wired correctly and says very little about whether it would catch real interference — the incidents staged here all sit well above the detection threshold by construction, so nothing in the corpus is a genuinely difficult case. Generating a body of hours with incidents at the edge of detectability is the experiment that would turn this from a consistency result into an accuracy one, and it is named here as out of scope rather than as unnecessary.
Then
A third way of noticing an abnormal reading, measuring how far a value sits from its own recent history rather than against a fixed threshold. It carries a prediction made earlier and never properly scored: that adding a new kind of test should touch almost nothing outside itself. The hours are now stable enough for that claim to be measured rather than asserted.
After
Telling somebody when a batch fails, and letting one be stopped — the two things the scenario screen still cannot do. Both are small beside detection, and both are honesty rather than capability, which is why they are written down rather than deferred silently.
Not planned
Making the third test work. It would need the hours made a third time, so that a coverage gap becomes an event with a place rather than five aircraft scattered across one. That is the change that would work, and it is declined on cost: every figure in the detection sections would have to be measured again. Recorded here rather than left as an open question, because an unstated "we could have" is worth less than a stated "we chose not to, and here is what it would have taken".
